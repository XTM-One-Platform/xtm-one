    Presentations

    A presentation is a slide deck your assistant builds for you, in a conversation, from a plain-language request. It renders right inside the chat, plays like a real deck (keyboard, touch, fullscreen, speaker notes), stays editable by asking for changes, and exports to PDF and PowerPoint that look exactly like the slides on screen.

    Every deck you can see is listed under Work > Presentations in the sidebar — your own, the ones colleagues shared with you, and the ones published to the whole company. The Work group is where what your agents produce lives: their runs under Operations, their decks here, and every other file they output or you gave them under Files.

    Create a deck

    Ask any assistant that can generate presentations — the General Assistant, or an agent with the presentation tool enabled — for slides, in your own words:

    Create a 10-slide all-hands deck for Q3: highlights, the three numbers that matter, the roadmap for Q4, and a closing slide with my contact details.

    The assistant composes the slides, and the deck appears in the conversation as an interactive card. You can also start from the Presentations page: New presentation opens the chat with the beginning of a request already typed for you.

    Brief it like a designer

    A good brief names the audience, the one message to remember, and the numbers you want on screen. The assistant chooses the slide compositions — a two-column comparison, a wall of key figures, a before/after, a diagram with an explanation, a roadmap, a takeaways slide — and keeps each slide readable from the back of the room. Ask for a short outline first when the deck is long, then let it build the whole thing in one pass.

    Slides can carry text, bullet and check lists, callouts, key figures, tables, feature grids, timelines, process flows, numbered cards, quotes, images, logo grids, and diagrams drawn from a text description (flowcharts, sequences, mind maps). The assistant can highlight a word or a phrase with bold, emphasis, code style, or a colored tone — for example an accent to point at the one thing that matters, or success / warning / error for a verdict.

    Sales and marketing collateral has its own building blocks: a feature matrix (a comparison table with an accented "us" column and check / cross marks), editions and plans as tier cards with one recommended tier and a call-to-action button each, a fact sheet made of badges (certifications, hosting regions, community numbers) and label / value rows, personas — two to four audience cards naming the role, what hurts today and what changes for them — and a banner for a hero header or a closing call to action with buttons that link to a page or an e-mail address. Ask for "a battlecard against a legacy TIP", "the editions slide", "who it is for" or "a closing slide with a demo button" and the assistant reaches for them.

    Pages, not only slides

    Not everything is presented from a stage. Ask for a product brief, a one-pager, a datasheet or a case study and the assistant lays it out on a portrait page — A4 or US Letter — instead of a 16:9 slide: a hero banner, a fact sheet, a feature matrix, the editions, and a call to action, denser than a slide because it is read, not projected. A page exports to a PDF on real A4 / Letter paper and to a portrait PowerPoint of the same size. On screen it is not a slide show — see Read a page.

    Images come from the predefined assets of the deck's template rather than from the assistant's imagination: the Filigran Brand Deck ships the real XTM platform architecture, the OpenCTI and OpenAEV product flows, product screenshots, certification badges and team photos, and three asset libraries hold the partner, integration and customer logos. Ask for "the XTM architecture diagram" or "a logo wall of our integrations" and the assistant places the actual figure — every one is described so it can be picked by what it shows. See Templates for the full inventory.

    Play a deck

    The card in the chat is a live deck. Everything you operate it with sits in the toolbar along its top edge — nothing is drawn over the slide itself: no corner arrows, no progress bar, no counter on the slide.

    • The middle of the bar is navigation: previous / next, and the slide counter (3 / 12), which opens a go-to-slide menu listing every slide by title, with a number field for long decks.
    • Present — the one highlighted button on the bar — takes the deck, toolbar included, to the whole screen. The bar keeps a strip along the top of the screen and the slide is laid out below it, so it never covers the slide — a title set right against the top edge stays visible while the bar is up. After a moment of stillness the bar slides away, leaving that strip black like the rest of the letterbox, and comes back as soon as you move the pointer; press Esc (or the bar's exit button) to leave. The keyboard is always active while presenting. Where the browser will not hand over the screen — a locked-down kiosk, an embedded desktop window — the deck fills the application window instead, with the same bar and the same keys; and from a chat embedded in another product, Present opens the standalone viewer, which can go fullscreen.
    • Open in studio, on the card in the chat, lands on the standalone viewer at /presentations/<id> — the same page a deck opens on from the Presentations list, with the editor, the comments, the history and the sharing controls — and opens the studio beside it, carrying this very conversation along so the assistant keeps everything it already knows about the deck. It is also the page you put on a projector or share as a link: no product menus, only the deck's own toolbar. Open in a new tab, in the More menu, opens that page beside the chat.
    • Comments opens the review notes on the deck and History its revisions (see Work on a deck together); the count on Comments is the number of notes still open. The initials beside Present are whoever else has the deck open right now.
    • The More menu holds the rarer presenter actions: the slide overview, the black screen, the speaker view with your notes and the next slide, the printable notes handout, and the list of keyboard shortcuts.

    In the chat, the keyboard belongs to the page until you click the deck or its toolbar; from then on the deck has the keys, until you click somewhere else. In the viewer and while presenting the deck always has them.

    Keys What they do
    Right Space N Page Down / Left P Page Up Next / previous slide
    Home / End First / last slide
    G Go to a slide
    O Slide overview
    F / Esc Present / stop presenting (Esc also closes the overview)
    B or . Black screen
    S Speaker view
    ? or F1 Keyboard shortcuts

    Shortcuts pause while you type in a field or use a menu, so a key never turns the page by accident.

    The deck is authored at a fixed 16:9 slide size (or 4:3 if you asked for it) and scales to whatever space it is given — a chat card, a laptop screen, a projector — so what you rehearse is what the room sees.

    Present with your notes

    Every slide can carry speaker notes — what you say over it, not what the room reads. The assistant writes them with the deck (ask for "speaker notes on every slide, conversational, two or three sentences each" and it does; the note under each slide in the editor panel shows the words and the speaking time it adds up to), and reports back which content slides still have none. They live in the deck, travel with it in the PowerPoint export, and are read from the speaker view.

    S, or Speaker view in the More menu, opens the speaker view in a window of its own — from the standalone viewer or from the card in the chat alike. Put the deck on the room's screen and keep this window on your laptop:

    • The current slide on top, so you know what the room sees without turning round — with a "screen is black" notice while the deck is paused.
    • Your notes in large type under it, the reason the window exists: bullets as bullets, emphasis as emphasis. + / - set the type size once and it is remembered.
    • The next slide in the side column, so the transition is spoken before it appears.
    • A stopwatch that starts on your first slide change (pause, resume, reset at will), the wall clock, and your pacing: the notes give every slide an estimated speaking time, so the window knows how far ahead of or behind the plan you are, and how much spoken time is left. Type a target length in minutes and the plan is re-scaled to the slot you actually have.
    • The transport: previous / next, a go-to-slide menu, the black screen — every one with the same key as the deck.

    The window follows the deck: whichever screen is playing it — the viewer, a chat card, the fullscreen stage — announces where it is, and the keys pressed in the speaker window move the room's screen. Your clicker moves both. With no deck window open it runs in rehearsal mode, and says so: same notes, same timer, its own position — the way to practise the talk before the room is there. Edits made from the studio while you rehearse land in the notes and the previews without a reload.

    Notes handout, in the same menu (and in the speaker window's header), opens every slide with its notes as one printable document at /presentations/<id>/notes — the sheets for the lectern, or for the colleague who presents the deck in your place. Only slides with notes leaves the section dividers out; print it to paper or to PDF from the browser.

    If the speaker window does not open

    Browsers block windows a page opens without a click behind them. Allow pop-ups for this site and press S again — the speaker view is a new window, never a popup drawn over the deck.

    Read a page

    A portrait presentation — a brief, a one-pager, a datasheet on A4 or Letter — is a document, and the platform treats it as one. Instead of a stage it opens as a reader: every page stacked on a scrolling surface as a sheet of paper, the same toolbar along the top edge, and nothing that only makes sense for a slide show — no fullscreen, no transitions, no speaker view.

    • The middle of the bar is navigation: previous / next page, and the page counter (1 / 2), which opens a go-to-page menu listing every page by its heading. Scrolling moves the counter too — the current page is the one under the top of the reader.
    • The zoom controls sit beside it: zoom in / out in steps, and Fit width, which sizes each sheet to the reader. Ctrl + mouse wheel zooms as well.
    • Open in studio, on the card in the chat, lands on the standalone reader at /presentations/<id> with the studio open beside it and the document's actions (edit, comments, history, share, export) alongside; Open in a new tab in the More menu opens it beside the chat.
    Keys What they do
    Right N Page Down / Left P Page Up Next / previous page
    Home / End First / last page
    G Go to a page
    + / - / 0 Zoom in / zoom out / fit width
    ? or F1 Keyboard shortcuts

    What you see is what prints: each sheet on screen is the page the PDF export produces, scaled as a whole, so a line that fits on screen fits on paper. In the library, pages carry a paper badge (A4, LETTER) and open with Open rather than Present; the Decks / Pages pills above the list show one kind at a time.

    Change a deck

    You never rebuild a deck from scratch. Ask the assistant for the change in the same conversation, and it changes the deck in place: the card in the chat and the link you already shared show the new version, and the viewer keeps your current slide instead of jumping back to the first one.

    Move the roadmap before the numbers, add a slide comparing the two options, and make the closing slide warmer.

    A small change stays small. "Change revenue to ARR on slide 12", "shorten this title", "swap the two columns", "delete the second bullet" edits exactly that slide, block or word: the assistant applies the change to the deck as it is stored rather than writing the whole deck again, so the other slides are not redrawn, reworded or reshuffled along the way — on a forty-slide deck as much as on a five-slide one. The reply says which slides changed and confirms that the rest are untouched, and the deck's History records the same thing (Edited slide 12). The layout check that follows every save looks at the slide that was edited — if the new wording no longer fits, the assistant trims that slide, not the others — while anything that was already tight elsewhere is left for you to raise, or for the viewer's Layout check; a change to the whole deck (a new theme, another aspect ratio) is checked on every slide, since every slide was redrawn. When two people edit the same deck, the second edit is applied to the deck as the first one left it — and if slides were added or reordered in between, the assistant re-reads the deck before touching a slide by its number, rather than editing whatever slide now sits there. Only a request that is genuinely a rewrite — a new outline, a different story — regenerates the deck.

    Point at the slide

    Naming the slide ("on slide 12", "the closing slide", or, in the studio, "this slide") is what keeps an edit surgical. A word that appears on several slides is changed only where you pointed; without a pointer, the assistant is shown every slide the word appears on before anything changes, so it changes them all on purpose or asks you which one you meant.

    Small last-minute fixes need no assistant round-trip. In the standalone viewer, Edit slides opens a side panel where you can:

    • reorder or delete a slide
    • correct a title or a subtitle
    • rewrite the speaker notes
    • switch the theme or the slide transition

    Anything structural — a new slide, a different composition, a chart — is the assistant's job, and the place for it is the studio.

    Edit in the studio

    Studio, in the viewer toolbar, opens a chat beside the deck: the conversation on the left, the presentation on the right as a live preview. Edit with my assistant, on a card in the library, lands on the same screen.

    • The assistant you talk to already knows which presentation is open — its title, its slides, the revision you are looking at, and what you are allowed to do with it. There is nothing to load and no identifier to paste: "make the title shorter", "add a slide comparing the two options after this one", "warm up the closing" is enough. It also knows which slide is on your screen, so "this slide", "here" and "it" mean the one you are looking at.
    • Pick any of your chat agents from the menu at the top of the rail, or stay with the General Assistant. Whichever you pick has the presentation tools while it is in the studio, even if it does not carry them elsewhere — you never have to find "the agent that can edit slides" first.
    • Every change is applied to this presentation, in place: the deck on the right refreshes the moment the assistant saves, the link you shared stays the same, and the change lands in the deck's History like any other edit. The assistant only creates a new presentation if you ask for a copy.
    • The rail is a full conversation: attach a file (a brief, numbers, a logo) with the paperclip, send a follow-up while the assistant is still working to steer it, and scroll up for earlier turns. If the deck came out of a conversation, the empty rail offers to continue that conversation, so nothing the assistant learned while building it is lost.
    • Make the rail as wide as you read. Drag the divider between the chat and the deck to widen the rail — up to half the screen, for the outline of a whole deck or a slide-by-slide review that would otherwise wrap every few words — and back again; the deck rescales as you drag. Double-click the divider to return to the default width. The divider works from the keyboard too (focus it, then Left / Right, Shift for bigger steps, Home / End for the narrowest and the widest). The width you pick is remembered in your browser for every presentation; it is not part of the link you share.
    • Collapse the rail with the chevron to give the deck the whole width; a thin Studio strip stays at the left edge to bring it back, with the conversation exactly where you left it. Present leaves the studio first — a chat beside a fullscreen deck would be invisible. While the rail is open, colleagues who have the deck open see you as editing.
    • Someone who can only view the deck can open the studio too: their assistant can walk them through it or make them a copy of their own to work on, but it cannot change the original — the rail says so before they type.
    • The studio remembers the conversation in the page address (?studio=1&c=…), so reloading, bookmarking or pasting the link to yourself reopens the same chat beside the same deck.

    The keyboard follows the focus: arrows typed in the composer never turn the page, and the deck's shortcuts work again as soon as you click back on it.

    Two people, one deck

    If a colleague or their assistant changes the deck while your edit panel is open, saving does not overwrite their work. The panel tells you the presentation changed and offers to reload the latest version before you apply your change again. The studio is subject to the same rule from the other side: the assistant reads the current revision before it edits, so a collaborator's change made a minute ago is not silently rewritten.

    Share a deck

    A deck starts private to you. Share (on the card in the chat, in the viewer toolbar, or on the library card) opens the sharing dialog. Access is granted in the dialog, never by the link on its own:

    • share with specific people or groups, choosing Can view or Can edit for each
    • administrators can publish the deck to the whole company (optionally restricting it to the people and groups listed)
    • then copy the link to paste into a message

    Under the link, Who can open this link says in words who it will open for — only you, the people and groups listed, or everyone in the company — and, while you have unsaved changes, that the new audience applies when you save. Anyone else who follows the link is told the presentation is not available.

    Someone who can edit can open the deck, use Edit slides, and ask their own assistant to change it in place from the studio — the link stays the same for everyone. Someone who can view can open, present, export, duplicate and comment on it; their Copy and edit with my assistant action works on a copy of their own, and the original is untouched.

    Sharing never changes the slides: opening the dialog while someone's assistant is mid-edit cannot disturb their work.

    Work on a deck together

    A shared deck is reviewed where it is presented. Three things in the viewer's toolbar are there for the people working on it with you:

    Who is here. The initials beside Present are the people looking at the deck right now — hover one for the name, and for whether they are editing and which slide they are on. They appear as someone opens the deck and disappear within a minute of them leaving.

    Comments. Comments opens a side panel of review notes. A note is attached to the slide you are on (or to the whole deck, if you say so), so a reviewer can write "this number is last quarter's" on the slide it concerns; clicking a note takes the deck to that slide. Anyone the deck is shared with can read and add notes and reply to them — reviewing is exactly what read-only access is for; you can edit and delete your own, and whoever manages the deck can tidy up any of them. When a note has been dealt with, Resolve moves it out of the way (the author or a manager decides) — Open and Resolved at the top of the panel switch between the two, and This slide narrows the list to the current slide. The count on the Comments button (and on the card in the library) is the number still open.

    Your assistant works the comments with you. In the deck's conversation, ask it to "address the review comments" and it reads each open note with the slide it is about and changes the deck in place; ask it to "implement Ada's comment on slide 5 and close it" and it makes the change, then resolves that note — with a reply saying what was done, if you like. It can also leave notes for you ("comment on every slide where a number has no source"), read the resolved ones back, or reopen a thread that was closed too early. Everything it posts appears under your name, in the panel, the moment it is saved, and it can only close what you could close yourself: on a deck you merely view, it will reply to a note rather than resolve it. Nothing is resolved unless you ask — a reviewer asked a question, and you decide when it is answered. A note on a slide that was later deleted stays in the panel, marked Slide removed, so nothing a reviewer wrote is lost.

    Writing a note yourself, Generate with AI under the text box drafts it for you: say what the note should convey ("ask for the source of the revenue figure") and the assistant writes it against the slide you are on — it sees the slide's content, not just your words — or against the thread you are replying to. If you have already typed a draft, the same button becomes Improve with AI and tightens what you wrote. The draft lands in the text box for you to edit and post, or discard; nothing is sent until you press Comment.

    The panel is as wide as you read: drag its left edge to widen it — up to a third of the screen — and back again, or double-click the edge to return to the default width; the divider works from the keyboard too (focus it, then Left / Right, Home / End). The width you pick is remembered in your browser for the editor, history and comments panels alike.

    History. Every save — an assistant generation, an edit in the panel, a restore — is kept as a numbered revision, with who made it, when, and what changed (Edited slides, title), the last thirty per deck. History opens the list; clicking a revision previews it in place, with a banner naming what you are looking at and Back to current beside it. Restore brings a revision back as the current version, and is itself a new revision, so a restore can be undone the same way. Restoring, like editing, needs edit access.

    Working alone, none of this gets in your way: the buttons are quiet until someone leaves a note or a second person opens the deck.

    Export a deck

    Export is available on the chat card and in the viewer toolbar. Every export is rendered by the platform itself from the same slides you see, so the result does not depend on your browser or zoom level:

    Format What you get
    PDF (vector) One page per slide, with selectable text and the deck's own fonts. Page size matches PowerPoint's slide size, so a PDF page and a PowerPoint slide of the same deck have identical dimensions.
    PowerPoint (pixel-perfect) One full-slide image per slide, with the speaker notes attached. Opens anywhere and looks exactly like the screen.
    PowerPoint (editable shapes) Text boxes, cards, tables, badges, buttons and dividers as real PowerPoint shapes placed where the platform placed them — tinted columns, check marks and outlined display numbers included — so a colleague can keep editing in PowerPoint. Icons, diagrams, logos and gradient surfaces are embedded as images.

    Print to PDF (browser), in the More menu of the card and of the viewer, is the fallback that needs nothing from the server: it opens the deck in your browser's print layout and starts the print dialog. In that dialog pick Save as PDF as the destination: the browser then uses the deck's own page size and every slide lands on a landscape page. Sending it to a printer instead - including a virtual one such as Microsoft Print to PDF - uses that printer's paper (usually portrait Letter or A4), so the slides come out rotated and boxed in the middle of each page.

    Which PowerPoint export?

    Use pixel-perfect when the deck is finished and only has to be presented from PowerPoint. Use editable shapes when someone will keep working on it there.

    Move a deck to another platform

    The last entry of the Export menu, JSON package (import elsewhere), is not a rendering: it is the deck itself — its slides, the name of its template and every logo, diagram or image it uses, embedded in one <title>.presentation.json file. Import on the Presentations page reads that file back and creates the deck under your name, so a deck built on a staging or local platform continues on production, and a deck can be kept as a backup outside the platform.

    On import, the platform first looks for what it already has: a template with the same name (the Filigran Brand Deck of every install, or a company template you recreated) and, inside it, the assets by filename — so a deck that uses the brand logos points at the destination's own logos and nothing is duplicated. Only what is missing is copied out of the file, as files that belong to you. The confirmation says what happened: how many assets were matched, how many were copied, and which template the deck ended up on. If the template does not exist on the destination, the install's default template is used and the message says so; if a file can be neither matched nor copied, the import is refused naming it, rather than silently producing a deck with a hole in it.

    The imported deck is a new deck — revision 1 in its history, with none of the original's shares or comments — and validates like any other: a package written by a newer platform whose slides use a layout the destination does not know yet is refused with the exact slide and field. A package embeds only the files you could download when exporting; a shared deck exports the files its owner shared with the deck, never anything else of theirs.

    Themes and brand templates

    A deck is styled by a template — a palette, fonts, logos and composition guidance. Out of the box every platform ships with the Filigran Brand Deck, a read-only template built from the Filigran design system, with the Filigran and product logos, the platform diagrams, product screenshots and badges as assets; it is what your decks look like unless the assistant is told otherwise, and an administrator can set another company template as the default in Settings > Parameters.

    To use a different template, name it in your request ("in the Sales template") — the assistant looks it up — or ask for no template at all to get the plain built-in dark or light look. Creating your own templates, including from a brief and a logo with Generate with AI, is covered in Templates.

    The Presentations page

    Work > Presentations in the sidebar lists every deck you can see, most recently changed first. Search by title, and narrow the list to Mine, Shared or Company. Each card shows the slide count, the theme, the owner, when it last changed, and - as icons on the right of the attribute row - whether it is shared with you, company-managed, or view only. Click anywhere on a card to open the deck. The card's footer carries Present (or Open for a page), Edit with my assistant (which opens the deck with the studio beside it), and a menu with Share, Duplicate, and Delete for a deck you manage. Import, next to New presentation, reads a JSON package exported from another platform (see Move a deck to another platform).

    Deleting a deck removes it for everyone it was shared with; a chat message that embedded it then shows the deck as no longer available.
