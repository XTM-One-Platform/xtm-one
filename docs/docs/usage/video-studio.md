# Video Studio

The Video Studio makes videos of every kind - product demos, but also brand films, event and booth loops, launch announcements, threat explainers and social teasers - and mixes everything a film is made of: big statements revealed line by line, animated AI shots generated by the video models of your AI providers, extracts of your own footage (an event film, a keynote), demos recorded in the platform's own browser, pictures, slides, title and closing cards - narrated or silent, captioned, branded and rendered to MP4. You make it with your assistant, by hand in the editor, or both at once: the assistant sits beside the stage, and everything it can change, you can change too.

Every video, recording and AI shot you can see is listed under `Work > Videos` in the sidebar.

!!! note "Turned on per deployment"
    The Video Studio is off until an administrator turns it on (`ENABLED_FEATURES=VIDEO_STUDIO`, see [Configuration](../deployment/configuration.md#video-studio)): until then `Work > Videos`, the Video Studio tool and the video templates are not offered.

## Start a video

`New video` asks for one thing: what the video is for. It makes any video, for work or just for fun.

1. **What kind of video?** (optional). Creative: `Just for fun`, `Short story`, `How-to`, `Greeting` (a birthday, a thank-you, a farewell). For work: `Product demo`, `Brand film`, `Event`, `Launch`, `Threat explainer`, `Social teaser`. The kind opens the request with its words and shows an example brief (`Use this example` copies it). A kind for work also suggests the template made for it and the format it starts in (vertical for a social teaser); a creative kind stays in free style.
2. **Describe it**: who watches, how long, what it should show - "3 minutes about the company, with extracts of our latest get-together, our events and product demos", or "a 30-second video of a cat astronaut on a candy planet, upbeat music, no narration".
3. **Look**: free style until you or a kind ask for a template - no brand, no added cards, nothing locked. `Change` lists the templates; a look you choose stays yours when you pick a kind afterwards, and the dialog offers the kind's template in one click (`Use it`).
4. **Format**: `Landscape` 16:9, `Mobile` 9:16, `Square` 1:1 or `Feed` 4:5 - the ones the chosen template makes.
5. **Made by**: the assistants with the Video Studio tool, your default agent first when it has the tool, otherwise the `General Assistant`.

The box at the bottom shows the exact request the assistant receives - the kind of video, then your words. `Create with the assistant` creates the video (untitled: the assistant names it), opens it in the editor and sends that request as the first message of the assistant's conversation, bound to the new video: it plans the scenes, starts the AI shots, writes the statements and the narration and fills the timeline while you watch. `Start from a blank video` opens a title card to build on by hand instead. Both create what the dialog shows: a blank video is in free style unless you chose a look, and a video just created opens with a clean quality check, whatever the template.

You can also ask any agent with the Video Studio tool in a normal chat ("Make a 60-second demo of creating a case from an alert in our demo OpenCTI, with a voice-over", "Make a 45-second silent loop for our booth"); its video appears as a card in the conversation and opens in the editor.

!!! tip "Brief it like a director"
    Name the audience, the one thing the viewer should remember, the length and where it plays (a booth screen, a feed, a meeting). For a demo, name the capabilities to show: the assistant narrates them as a product manager presenting live would - what each one does and gives, never the clicks or what the screen already reads - and the platform keeps the camera still unless a detail is too small to read, draws the cursor with click ripples, compresses the waits and paces every scene on its narration. For a brand film or an event loop, give the message and the material: the assistant writes it as statements over AI shots, extracts of your footage and the brand's own pictures.

The assistant's know-how comes from the **Video Production** skill: the arc of each kind of video, how to write a statement and an AI shot prompt, how to use real footage, script a recording of your product and narrate a demo as a product manager would, how to build an explainer from your intelligence, to fix what the quality check finds after each save, to let shots and renders finish in the background instead of making you wait, and to generate a failed shot again rather than swap in a still image without telling you. The `General Assistant` carries it out of the box, and so do, on XTM One, the `CTEM Assistant`, the `XTM-One Assistant` and the assistant of each product (`OpenCTI Assistant`, `OpenAEV Assistant`, `OpenCRQ Assistant`, `FIMI Analyst`), together with the Video Studio tools; attach it to your own agents like any skill (the General Assistant's skills are set under `Settings > General Assistant > Skills`, see [Settings](../administration/settings.md#general-assistant-skills)).

## The editor

A video opens in its editor, full screen:

- **At the top, the header**, the same bar as a presentation's: the back arrow to your videos, the title (click it to rename the video), `Play` (watches the current render, full screen - see [Watch a video](#watch-a-video)), `Render` (opens the `Renders` tab), undo and redo, `Studio` (shows or hides the assistant), `Share`, `Download` and the menu. Until the video is rendered, `Play` says so.
- **On the left, the assistant**: a chat bound to this video. It knows which video you are editing and which scene the playhead is on, so "tighten the narration of this scene" or "add a callout on the Create button here" needs no explanation. Its saves land on the stage immediately. `Open in chat` continues the conversation in the main chat; the history menu lists the conversations about this video. Collapse it to give the stage the room; `Studio` in the header, or the strip left at the edge, brings it back.
- **In the centre, the preview**: the video as it will render, drawn by the same compositor the render uses, from your draft, live. Recordings and AI shots play from their footage, so you see the camera, the cursor, the transitions, the overlays and the captions as they will be. The narration is spoken with the video's voice: a new or changed line is spoken in the background a few seconds after you stop editing (its block on the narration track pulses meanwhile, and the line is paced at reading speed until it is ready), then plays at its real length. The render reuses the voice you heard and does not pay for it again. Deck slides are drawn at render time and show as a card naming the slide. The preview stays steady while the assistant works on the video: what is on screen keeps playing through its saves, a shot generated again keeps showing its current take until the new one can play, a shot seen for the first time shows its poster while its footage loads, and the next scene is loaded before it comes on screen.
- **The transport** under it: play and pause, frame by frame, scene by scene, to the start or the end, the playback speed, loop, mute and full screen.
- **The timeline**: every scene where the render will place it, named by its title or first line, else by the recording or the AI shot it plays (`in progress` while it is still being made) - transitions show as the overlap they are, a narration longer than its scene shows the scene stretched to it - with lanes for the overlays, the narration and the music. Click a scene to select it, drag the ruler to move the playhead, drag a scene to reorder it, and use the toolbar to cut at the playhead, duplicate, delete, move, zoom and add a scene. A template's intro and outro are drawn too, locked: they belong to the template. Drag its top edge to make it taller (up to a little over half the screen) or shorter; double-click the edge to reset it.
- **On the right, the inspector**:
    - `Scene`: everything about the selected scene - a card's title, subtitle and style; a statement's lines, kicker, figure, smaller lines, chips, reveal and what plays behind it; a recording's or a shot's trim (start and end), speed and held last frame; the narration and when it starts; the transition in; the overlays (lower thirds, callouts, badges, captions, spotlights and pictures - the brand's logo or mark, at a width and an opacity) at their time or on a recording step. A recording also has its post-production: idle moments (speed them up, cut them out, or keep them), the camera (follow the action, still, or keyframes; the deepest zoom; what it zooms on; its pace - `Calm`, the default, moves smoothly, `Dynamic` quicker for a short social cut - and how long a zoom takes, to slow every move down or speed it up), the cursor (size, style, click effect), the browser frame (window or full bleed, address bar, margin, corners, shadow) and step highlights. Following the action, the camera opens wide, zooms in on a step, re-frames on the next one (pulling back on the way when it travels far), backs out when a step opens another page or before a scroll, and zooms in again; a highlighted region is framed whole.
    - `Video`: the title, format and frame rate, the template or free style, the theme (colour scheme, backdrop, accents, logo, watermark), the voice-over (speech model, voice, delivery, pace, volume, pronunciations), the captions, the music (a built-in track, an AI track or an upload, and how it sits under the voice) and the default transition.
    - `Media`: add statements, title and closing cards, AI shots (generate one here, it joins the video while it generates), recordings, and images or video files from your computer. `In this video` lists the recordings and AI shots the video uses; `Your library` finds any other one by search (the most recent show until you type), to use it here too. A failed shot is not listed: it shows in its scene, with why and `Generate again`.
    - `Renders`: render the video and follow, play, download or cancel its renders.

    Drag the inspector's left edge to widen it, up to a third of the screen, like the assistant's panel on the left.

A video shared with you as a viewer says `View now` on its card in the library rather than `Open in the editor`, and plays its current render across the page, as its public link does: the video with its voice, music and captions, its length, its size and when it was rendered. Until it is rendered, the preview, its transport and the timeline take the whole screen instead, under a line saying it is a preview of the edit. The header keeps the way back, the title, `Play` and `Download` once the video is rendered, and the menu (copy the link, duplicate the video). There is no assistant, no inspector, no undo and no editing tool on the timeline. Duplicate the video to edit a copy of your own.

A setting left empty uses the template's value (or the default); a setting the template locks is shown with a lock and cannot change. The title and closing cards a template adds are drawn dashed on the timeline: select one and `Customize the opening card` (`Customize the closing card`) gives the video its own copy, with the template's words, that you edit like any scene - its words, its length, its style, the logo - while it keeps the template's look for what you leave empty. To change only how long a card the template adds lasts - what the quality check asks when its words are on screen too briefly - or the small line above its title (its kicker: other words, or none at all), the assistant sets it on the video (`template_scenes`) and the card stays the template's. Every save says the shortest each of these cards can last with its words still read ("template_scenes.outro.duration at least 5.2 s for its 8 words"), so a shorter length is known before a save refuses it. A card shows its brand once, in its logo: a kicker the template adds is left out when it would only repeat the name the logo already spells. Every edit saves itself a moment after you stop; `Ctrl+Z` undoes it (an edit the assistant saved too), and a problem the video cannot be saved with is shown under the header without losing your draft. After each save, the **quality check** line under the header lists what the audience would see go wrong, if anything (see [Good to watch by construction](#good-to-watch-by-construction)); click a finding to open its scene. When an update of the platform changes what the check finds, an editor left open reads it again rather than keep showing the findings and advice of the older check. `Fix with my assistant`, on that line, opens the assistant beside the stage with the findings written in its message box, the way the assistant reads them after its own saves: read them, add to them if you want, and send. This is the way to act on a finding you cannot fix by hand, such as an AI shot to generate again.

| Key | Does |
|---|---|
| `Space` | Play / pause |
| `Left` / `Right` | Previous / next frame |
| `Shift+Left` / `Shift+Right` | Previous / next scene |
| `Home` / `End` | To the start / the end |
| `S` | Cut the scene at the playhead |

| `L` / `M` / `F` | Loop / mute / full screen |

## Statements, footage and pictures

A video is more than its recordings:

- **Statements** carry the message in big type, the way booth and launch films do: up to four short lines revealed one after the other (or word by word, or all at once), a kicker above, a figure counted up on screen ("6,000+"), smaller lines under them (a proof point, a translation) and chips ("Open source", "Community led"). Words between asterisks - `Too much *noise*.` - are drawn in the template's accent colour; the same works in the title of a title or closing card, where a line break you type (`Enter` in the editor) starts a new line - `Uncover threats.` over `*Take action.*`. Two asterisks are an accent only when they hug words: `5* rated, 4* loved` is drawn as typed. An asterisk that opens an accent no other closes (`We see *everything`) is drawn as typed too, and saving the video says which word to close, so the assistant fixes it before a render shows it. A statement sits on the template's backdrop, or over an AI shot, an uploaded video or an image, darkened so the words read (`Behind the text`, `Darken it`).
- **Extracts of your own footage**: upload an event film, a keynote or a customer testimonial as a video file (`Media > From your computer`) and keep its best seconds with `Starts at` / `Ends at`.
- **The brand's pictures**: a template carries the brand's pictures, and every built-in video template carries those of its brand's deck - the product logos and marks, diagrams and product screenshots on every platform, and on Filigran Copilot the team and event photos, badges and the partner, integration and customer logos too. The assistant places them as image scenes or statement backdrops, and animates them as figures (a wall of customer logos, an architecture whose boxes wear the product marks). Vector pictures (SVG logos, badges, diagrams) are used like any other.
- **A logo on a scene**: a picture overlay draws the brand's logo or mark (by its role in the template: the logo, its light variant, the mark) or any picture file - an emblem, a product mark - on an AI shot, a recording, a statement or a card, at a corner or wherever it fits, at a width and an opacity, for the time you set. It sits above the scene's picture and below the captions, and is placed like a badge: away from the scene's text and the subtitles. Ask the assistant ("put our mark in the top right corner of every AI shot") or add a `Picture` overlay in the inspector. A video model is never asked to draw a logo: it would misspell it. A template can add its mark to every scene of a kind by itself (its rules, `scene_defaults`); the built-in templates do not, their watermark already carries the mark.
- **A real picture inside a picture**: to show your real product on the screen drawn in a still (a booth screen, a monitor, a phone), or your real wordmark on a backwall, the assistant draws the screenshot or the logo into the picture - flat, or in perspective on the screen's four corners - and shows you the result before using it.
- **Your real screen inside a moving shot**: when an AI shot shows a monitor, a billboard or a poster, a screen insert puts your recording, another shot, a video file or a picture onto it, in perspective and frame by frame, so its words stay sharp at the quality you render at. It works on an AI shot or a video file, a recording, a picture scene (a still of a room with its monitors is the simplest way to lock a wall shot: its screens take inserts as a statement's backdrop does), or the backdrop of a statement, and can glow a little like a lit screen. Ask the assistant ("play my dashboard recording on the monitor of the booth shot"): it reads the screen's corners off the shot. It can also put the template's own logo there (the full logo, or the bare mark: two different files, the one named is the one drawn); a picture with transparency keeps it, `Fit inside the frame` keeps the picture whole with the surface showing around it (never a black card) - on a chroma-keyed screen the bands it leaves beside a screenshot or a recording are filled with the picture's own edge colour or the screen's black instead, so the green never shows there, while a logo on transparency keeps the board around it; left without a key on a green, blue or magenta screen, those bands show the screen's own colour, which fails the render (`key colour left in the contain bands`: set the key to that colour, or use `Fill the frame`) - and the glow halos the picture's own shape. What passes in front of the surface stays in front: ask the video model for a flat green screen and set the insert's `Chroma key` to `Green screen` (its tolerance and feather beside it), so the insert is drawn only where the screen shows green and a cat or a hand crossing it stays in front, its green fringe removed - hair included, whatever the feather, while a colour of what stands in front itself (a green-lit face, a green collar, the pink nose of an animal crossing the screen, however little of it a frame shows and however blurred by its motion) is kept and never counted as a fringe: those colours are read on the whole subject, over the whole shot, when you save. The screen's green that fur, hair or a sleeve carries on its own edge in the shot is taken off that edge too, even when the model painted its green leaning to cyan, without touching the colours inside it, and the edge then picks up a little of the inserted picture's light, as a real screen gives it; the insert's `despill` sets this (`edge`, on by default; `width_px`, how far past the edge it reaches, 2 by default; `strength`, 0-1; `own_hues`, colours of what stands in front to keep as its own, by name - `red`, `orange`, `yellow`, `green`, `cyan`, `blue`, `purple`, `magenta` - or as `#rrggbb`, e.g. `["magenta", "#e8a0b0"]`), `edge: false` leaving that edge as the shot shows it. When you save, the assistant is told how much of the key the edge of whoever stands in front holds on the frame the insert comes in on, and how much of it the despill leaves, before any preview - and, when what stands in front holds the screen's colour or its opposite (a pink nose in front of a green screen) over the shot, that this is its own colour, kept and never counted. `Green screen` (like blue and magenta) means the green the shot's screen actually shows - read off the frame the insert comes in on, since video models paint their own shade of it - so the inserted colours come out as they are; a custom `#rrggbb` key is used exactly as given; without a key, what enters the surface through its edge stays in front of it, while what the model drew on the screen itself is covered. Whatever stands in front is never drawn over, on any frame, keyed or not: a black cat over a dark screen stays whole in front of it even where it barely differs from the screen, because it is joined to the part of it that crossed the screen's edge; a subject already in front of the screen when the insert comes in stays in front too, the screen read clean over the whole time the insert shows; and a subject the key reads partly as the screen (a green-lit sweater) is never seen through the insert, only its edge blending into it. The answer says how sure that is for each insert: a subject told from the screen only by its link to what crossed the edge - dark on dark - reads as a low confidence, and a key matching the screen makes it sure. Under 50 % sure on more than 5 % of the insert's frames while the subject covers more than 2 % of the screen, the render fails as `insert ghost` with one fix: give the shot a green screen and the insert its key, or, on a keyed screen, a subject in colours far from the key. Without a key, what stands in front is read at the frame's own resolution around its edge - its soft fringe, a tuft of fur finer than the shot's grid - so no line of the inserted picture is drawn across it; a render that still shows the inserted picture's own lines across the subject fails as `insert seen through the foreground`, its frame and a 100 % crop attached. On a keyed screen, the key colour anywhere else in the shot - a glossy floor reflecting the green screen, a wall it lights, never another screen of the scene nor a colour of the subject itself - fails as `key spill outside the quad`, the whole frame attached with that colour marked; the inserted picture's own green inside the screen is by design. A keyed screen never blinks: on a still shot (`Follow the camera` off) the screen is filled once from its first frame rather than keyed again on every frame - what crosses it still stays in front, its edge softened by the key's feather, and the tolerance decides how much of the shot's green around the bezel the key takes -, and on a moving one the key is read over five frames together, so the edge of the insert does not jitter with the footage's compression. The glow is a soft halo in the picture's own colour, kept to the screen's bezel: it never lights the wall around the monitor, and a keyed screen glows only when you ask it to. `Room light` (15 % by default) is how much of the shot's own light and reflections play over the insert - a window's glare across the monitor, a lamp's falloff - laid on as light, never as a tint of the room's colour or of the green screen: a screen lit evenly adds nothing, and 0 draws the picture exactly as it is. The editor's preview plays the insert as the render draws it - keyed, following the camera, the recording moving - once it has been drawn in the background (the scene says it is being prepared meanwhile, and shows the inserted media mapped onto the surface); the scene's thumbnail in the timeline shows its first frame (never the green screen it covers); the inspector lists the insert with its fit, glow, room light, key and timing. On footage whose camera moves, turn on `Follow the camera` (or ask the assistant): the screen's corners, read off the frame the insert comes in on, are followed in perspective through the footage - by the surface and the frame around it - so the insert stays on its surface. A frame where that following is unsure (a plain wall, a screen whose own picture moves) keeps the screen's edges as that frame shows them, so a nearly still shot is followed from its first frame; saving the video snaps the corners to the screen's edges on that first frame and says how its frames were placed ("track: 190 frames by the tracker, 2 by the bezel read") or where the surface would be lost. When the surface is lost (the camera cuts away, something covers it), the render and the scene preview fail with `surface lost at t=...` and the frame where it was lost, rather than draw the insert off its surface. On an AI shot whose camera was locked off after it was made, the insert is followed through the shot as the model made it and placed where the locked-off shot shows the screen, so it does not jitter; when locking the camera cropped into the screen, the render fails as "screen cropped" at that frame instead of drawing half an insert (generate the shot again without stabilisation, and the insert follows its camera). A wall of screens is one insert: ask for several panes on the one surface ("show OpenCTI, OpenAEV and XTM One on the three screens of the wall"), side by side or in rows and columns, each showing its own picture or video with a thin gap between them; the panes follow the wall together, in its perspective, and share its key. The corners you give are a starting point: on every frame the render reads the edge of the screen's bezel around them and snaps the insert to it, so a quad a few pixels off still sits square on the monitor and follows it as the camera moves, and the insert is drawn sharp at the quality you render at (never drawn on the shot and enlarged with it). On a still shot you can leave the corners out altogether: when the video is saved, the screen's bezel is found on the frame the insert comes in on - the flat green, blue or magenta screen the shot shows, in the key's colour when the insert has one - and stored as the insert's corners, so the first preview already draws it on its bezel; the save is refused, that frame attached, only when it shows no such screen. Corners you give within 2 % of the frame's width of the bezel are snapped to it when the video is saved; further off, the save says so and you leave them out to take the bezel. One insert per scene can find its corners so, and an insert that follows the camera takes corners given. A curved screen - one whose edges bow by more than 5 % of their length, the points along the edge confirming it - cannot take a flat insert (each edge is judged only on the part nothing stands in front of: an edge mostly hidden by a person or an object is said as covered and not judged, so a subject standing at the bezel never makes a straight screen look curved): a shot meant to carry an insert that starts from a picture with such a screen is refused before anything is paid, a take that bends its screen fails, and the save says so of an insert on it - generate the shot again with a flat screen. A render that finds a screen curved where the shot's own check passed it on the same take says it `disagrees with` that check, as a warning that never holds the render. A flat screen seen from the side or from below is fine: the insert is drawn in its perspective exactly, and the assistant only says how far it is turned ("seen at 35 degrees"). Only a shot that carries an insert is checked - a screen replacement, a shot the assistant says will carry one, or a shot a scene already draws an insert on - so a mug or a lamp in an ordinary shot never stops it, and the assistant can say a shot carries no insert at all (then no screen is checked on it, and its stray screens are not cleaned). A rounded or glowing object of a screen's colour is never taken for one: a screen has four straight edges around a flat key colour or a dark fill, and is never the brightest thing in the picture. Under a chroma key the screen is painted flat behind the insert - nothing of the green shows through -, and it stays covered on every frame of its scene, both transitions included: before the insert comes in and after it goes, the screen shows as switched off (the bezel's dark colour, else black, the room's light on it), the insert fades in and out over that, a screen showing two inserts one after the other hands over from one to the next, and a cut or a fade to the next scene blends the two finished pictures - the green never shows, not even for a frame. An insert that lasts as long as its scene or longer never fades out: it stays whole to the scene's very last frame, however much longer the scene plays than planned (a cut moved onto the music's beat, a held last frame), through the fade into the next scene too. Every preview and render reads its own video back, frame by frame: a screen showing one flat colour, or a picture other than the insert's, on a frame where the insert should show fails as `insert missing`, with that frame. On a transition's frames - a crossfade into the next scene - the insert is blended with the scene coming in, so it is read with that scene taken out of the frame, never against the insert alone: a crossfade never fails an insert that is there, a screen that really went black still does, and the finding says how much of the insert the frame showed there (its alpha). Frames where less than half of it still shows are not judged, and the answer says how many. A transition that blurs or dims the whole picture - a blur, a fade_black, a zoom, a flash - or the video's fade from or to black blurs and dims the insert with it: there the screen is judged against the frame around it, so it fails only when it is flat while the rest of the picture keeps its detail, and the answer says how many of its frames it judged so. The matte is steadied from frame to frame so it never flickers, the key's shade and how unevenly the screen is lit are both read off the shot, and the green fringe on whoever stands in front is taken out without turning it magenta. A screen partly out of frame keeps its true corners outside it and the insert shows through the visible part: never squeeze the quad to the frame's edge, which skews the insert (the save says so as advice). The save also says when an insert's text would be too small to read: a recording, an uploaded screenshot or an uploaded video whose smallest text is drawn under 10 pixels of a 1080p frame is said as advice, under 6 pixels as a finding the render waits for - typically a wall of many panes, each showing a full-size recording. Both offer only what brings its text to 12 pixels and keeps the page's own layout: the share of the frame the insert needs (fewer panes on a wall), or a camera push on the insert - ask the assistant to "push in on the alerts panel": the insert's `zoom` draws its picture up to 4 times larger within its surface, easing in between two moments and panned to the region you name. A larger surface or a push is offered only at a size the picture stays sharp at in the quality you render the video at (its latest render's, `High` before the first), so a push the save suggests is never one the render refuses. When the picture cannot be drawn that large, the advice names what can: the pixel ratio to record it at again (the same layout, more pixels), the picture size to use, or, for a recording, the largest push it stays sharp at and the browser zoom to record it at again so its text is larger in the same pixels. A push sharp at 1080p that a 4K render would refuse says so. A picture drawn under 0.3 of its own size shows as a whole page - a dashboard on a wall screen -, which a push would crop: it is offered no push, and the advice says why. Recording it again with its text larger for its width - on a screen under 40 % of the frame's width the browser zoom (the page zoomed in, its window and frame keeping their size), on a wider one a narrower window - is suggested only when the recording's page keeps its layout at the width it would then be laid out at: once a recording is made, its page is narrowed step by step to find where its layout changes (a menu collapses, cards stack, the page scrolls sideways), and the recording says how far down it holds. A dashboard told to record again zoomed in, or in a window, too narrow for its desktop layout would come back as its mobile layout, cropped and over-zoomed on a wall screen. A recording asked at a browser zoom its page does not fit at is refused before anything is recorded: the assistant is told the width the page is laid out at, how far it overflows and the largest zoom that keeps its layout ("at zoom 190 the page lays out at 842 px and overflows by 225 px; the largest zoom that keeps its layout is 150"), and a page that overflows below its minimum width says that width exactly ("its page keeps this layout down to 1067 px"). A larger share of the frame is suggested only when the scene has it: two monitors side by side cannot each take 68 % of the frame, so when the inserts sharing its width would together need more than all of it, the advice says so and suggests what needs no more of the frame - a push, the recording made again at a higher browser zoom up to the largest its page keeps its layout at ("largest safe zoom 150 on this page"), or a tighter page, such as a single dashboard panel recorded on its own. How much the source is scaled down is given with it, but is not the test: a recording at pixel ratio 2 is drawn at under half its size and stays readable when its text is large enough. AI shots and logos are not judged, nor an insert that is scenery: three monitors sharing one frame show their recordings far too small to read by design, so the assistant marks each such insert `ambient` and the check gives the size its smallest text is drawn at as a fact ("ambient, not judged - its smallest text, about 24 px in the source, reads about 5 px on the 1080p frame"), rather than advise on it. Marking an insert so changes nothing it draws, and keeps the scene's preview. The advice always offers to mark an insert `ambient` when it is scenery. The assistant is told the advice once per insert - the panes of a wall together -, not on every save; the finding, every time. It says which resolution the numbers and the crops come from: a preview's are its draft's, and a 4K render measures and crops them again on its own 2160p frames. Recordings are made at a pixel ratio of 2 by default, sharp enough for a 4K render on most screens. The check for a soft insert runs when the video is saved and with every scene preview, for each quality you may render at (`standard`, `high` and `ultra`): a recording too small for its screen in a 4K render is said then ("soft at ultra"), with the size it needs, as advice that refuses nothing - a scene preview is drawn at 1080p and would never show it. A whole render at a quality where an insert would be soft is still refused before it is paid for. - **A screen partly hidden behind someone**: when a person or an object stands over one side of a keyed screen, so that side's bezel cannot be seen, the save places that side where the screen's key colour ends rather than at the person's edge, and says so ("right side hidden behind the foreground: placed at the key colour's extent, x 0.985"). An image scene or a statement's backdrop can also name the recording and its step (or a moment) directly: the frame is kept as a picture when the video is saved, once, and taken again only when the recording or the moment changes. A render of the video is a video file too: the assistant can look at any moment of a finished render before you call it final, whoever rendered it, as long as you can open the video.
- **Slides**: a deck slide plays as a picture with a camera motion; `chrome: false` (ask the assistant) draws it without the deck's footer, logo and slide number, for a slide shown inside the film rather than presented. When subtitles are shown over a slide, it is scaled to stand above them, so they never cover its footer.
- **Animated figures**: one diagram of a deck - an architecture, a process flow, a timeline, a roadmap's phases, a chart, a diagram, an org chart - drawn alone in the video's colours and built in step by step: an architecture's zones first, then each node on its own, row by row, each link drawn once both its ends are there; a process flow's steps and a roadmap's phases in order; chart series growing; an org chart level by level - the root, then each level's cards, then its teams' bubbles growing to their size, every headcount counting up, the dotted lines last. Each step fades in as it rises into place, and the camera moves to it before ending on the whole figure for the scene's last two seconds. Built, the figure keeps moving to the end of its scene: it drifts in slowly, a light pulse runs along each link from its source to its target, and the core node's glow breathes. The figure is framed on everything it draws - a label wider than its node included - and its drift is fitted to the frame: drifted and leaned or turned in space as it is drawn, it always keeps 3% of the frame clear on every side; where only a drift too small to see would, it holds still once built instead. When the assistant saves a video, it is told the drift each figure plays and how close to the frame's edge it comes. A figure taken from a deck follows its slide, and is drawn in the video's theme: a template logo the slide places for the other theme - a light deck's dark-on-light product logos on a dark video - is drawn as the twin its template declares for this one, so pointing a scene at another deck's slide is one edit, never a copy of the block with its logos swapped by hand; the assistant is told which logos it swapped. A template logo kept as a picture for the slides that already place it - soft past 1080p - and replaced by a vector version that its template names is drawn as that vector, in the video's theme: a light deck's `opencrq-logo-light.png` draws `opencrq-logo.svg` on a dark video, sharp at 4K. ## Motion design

Everything a video draws - its cards, statements, overlays and motion graphics - moves the brand's way, set once for the whole video under `Video > Motion`:

- **Personality**: `Precise` (crisp, measured moves - the platform's own motion), `Calm` (slow, soft reveals), `Energetic` (snappy springs, titles letter by letter), `Cinematic` (long blurred reveals and a light sweeping across the accent words) or `Playful` (bouncy springs). It sets every timing and easing at once: how a title comes in, how far a line rises, how fast the lines of a statement follow each other, how a card's logo appears. `Speed` makes the whole video's motion faster or slower, and `Titles come in by` overrides how titles split (lines, words or letters). A template presets its personality, and a brand template locks it.
- **Motion graphics**: a `Motion graphics` scene (`Add scene` in the timeline, or `Media > Cards`) lays out a whole animated scene from a few words - a kinetic title, the brand's logo revealed, a figure counted up ("6,000+"), a list built item by item, a statement split across windows, a quote, a title over a moving constellation - in the video's colours and personality, whatever its format: a 9:16 version of the same scene stays readable. Choose its `Composition` in the inspector and write its words. The assistant can also draw a motion scene of its own: text set letter by letter, shapes and lines drawn stroke by stroke, one shape turning into another, pictures and logos moving along a path, a 3D camera moving through the elements, particles, and Lottie animations exported from After Effects (Bodymovin JSON with its images embedded and its text as glyphs; a file that loads anything from outside itself is refused when the video is saved, and expressions are not played - the animation plays its keyframes).
- **On the beat**: with music, `Cuts on the beat` makes each scene play a little longer - never shorter, a second at most - so the cut lands on a beat; `Cuts and builds on the beat` also brings a statement's lines and a motion scene's elements in on the beats. A built-in score's beats are known from its tempo; an AI track or an uploaded sound is listened to once, and a sound with no steady beat is left as it is - a warning of the save, with the two ways out (a track with a steady beat, or beat sync off); so is beat sync on a video with no music.
- **Motion blur**: what moves blurs as it would on film. It is drawn at the `High` and `Ultra` qualities only, so previews stay fast, and a render with it takes about three times as long.
- **Figures in space**: an animated figure can stand in 3D while it builds (`In space`: leaning back and turning towards the camera, or turning slowly side to side).

Every frame is still drawn from the time alone, never from the frame before it: a scene preview, a render split across workers and a render drawn again show exactly the same motion.

## Good to watch by construction

Every frame is laid out so that nothing covers anything else, whatever the assistant (or you) wrote:

- **The subtitles keep their band.** While someone speaks, the text of a card or a statement, a lower third and a recording's browser window stay above the subtitles (below them when they are at the top) - burned in or not, because a player draws the subtitle track in that same band. A card's whole text, not just its title, is scaled down to fit the room left, and a word too long for the width (a URL) is scaled into it.
- **Overlays find their place.** A badge or a callout asked where the scene's text already is takes the first free corner; two callouts on one element stand on either side of it, or one above the other; a lower third stands above the subtitles.
- **Subtitles are readable.** A subtitle holds on two lines, set a little smaller when its sentence is long.
- **The watermark leaves the stage to the brand.** Title and closing cards already carry the brand: the corner watermark leaves while they play and comes back with the content, a moment after it starts and once the card has left the frame, however long the transition. A statement does not draw its logo while the video has a watermark: the watermark already shows the brand, and the logo shows again once the watermark is off. The brand mark is never drawn twice on one frame. The brand film templates open on a centred title card with the logo.

- **Transitions cut like an editor.** Left on `Automatic` (the default, and what every built-in template but the Filigran Product Video uses: a demo crossfades calmly from scene to scene), each cut is picked from the scenes on either side: the opening card pushes into the video, words dissolve into pictures and pictures into words, a run of AI shots dissolves from one to the next (or, at a `Dynamic` rhythm, alternates a push through the frame and a whip pan), the closing card comes after a dip to black, and a shot too short to dissolve is cut. `Video > Transitions` sets the rhythm - `Calm`, `Dynamic`, or `Classic`, which only cuts, crossfades and fades to black, the grammar of a brand film (the brand film templates use it) - or one transition for every cut - cut, crossfade, fade through black, slide, zoom, blur, wipe, whip or flash - and a scene's `Transition in` overrides it for that scene, which says what it comes in with. The assistant is told the transition planned into every scene each time it saves or reads the video, so it can check the cuts before rendering.
- **Footage keeps moving to the end of its scene.** A scene lasts as long as its narration. When the narration is longer than the AI shot, the animated picture or the video behind a statement, the footage plays slower to fill the scene, down to 70% of its speed. A shot whose own sound you keep plays at its speed, because slowing it would slow its sound. A recording first plays the pauses it sped up more slowly, never slower than twice real time, so a page loading still reads sped up; the steps keep their real speed, and pauses set to `Cut` stay cut. A recording still shorter than its narration is then slowed down by the save itself, to the speed at which it lasts as long, never below half speed: the assistant is told "speed set to 0.69 automatically" and has nothing to type back. Only a recording still too short at half speed is reported, with the seconds still missing (record more of the flow, or shorten the narration). Setting the scene's fit to `off` keeps the speed you chose. Whatever is still missing holds the last frame, drifting slowly in until the next scene, so the picture never stops dead before a transition. Your own `Hold the last frame` is kept as you set it: a still moment, which does not drift. The preview and the render play it the same way.
- **A demo is told as a product manager tells it.** On the Filigran Product Video template a recording keeps its camera still - a scene asking for a detail moves it calmly, no deeper than 1.3 times, in moves of 2 seconds -, the scenes crossfade calmly, a callout stays 3 seconds and the opening card is centred (`left` on the card the template adds, under `template_scenes`, leaves the right of the frame to the backdrop). Every save holds each recording scene to it, as advice that never blocks a render: a narration that reads its screen - an action said as an instruction ("Click the layered button"), a count of what the screen draws ("23 entities"), a name of two words or more its frames show while it is said (each frame read once by your chat model; the brand's names and the video's title are not counted) -, an automatic camera making more than 4 moves a minute of its scene (one is always allowed) or moving in on an element covering over 40% of the page, and a recording slowed under 0.7 times to last as long as its narration, with the narration length that keeps it at 0.8 times.
- **A video can be dynamic by construction.** A video's pace set to `dynamic` (for a short social cut) presets the quick version of everything the video leaves unset: recordings cut their pauses instead of speeding them up, the camera moves quicker with half-second zooms, a recording keeps only 0.3 s after its last step, transitions cut at the dynamic rhythm, statements reveal their words one by one - and a recording longer than its narration is sped up to end with it, up to 1.25 times, the assistant told the speed it set. A scene can say its narration a little faster or slower than the rest (its own narration speed), and the voice-over's style can be the `energetic` or `calm` preset. Every save says how the video reads: words a minute of voice-over, the average and the longest scene, and how long each recording's screen stays still - the cursor still, the page unchanged - while the voice speaks; a scene over 20 seconds, or still for more than 3 seconds under its narration, is flagged with what to change.
- **A recording can fill the frame.** A recording scene set to `fill` is drawn edge to edge - no margin, no browser bar, no rounded corner or halo - for a screen whose details must be read; the subtitles still keep their band. A template that locks the browser window leaves it to each scene, unless it locks `fill` off.

What a layout cannot fix is measured: after each save, the video is played in the render's compositor at the moments that matter and checked for text covering text, text cut by or touching the edge of the frame, text scaled down so far it gets hard to read, text still appearing when its scene ends, words on screen too briefly to be read, subtitles too fast to follow, words over a backdrop not darkened enough, overlays too short to read, and the brand mark drawn twice on one frame (`brand mark drawn twice`: the watermark, a card's logo or a picture overlay of the brand's mark together, with the time of the frame and what to switch off to keep one). The assistant reads the findings with the result of every save and fixes them before it renders; you see them in the editor under the header.

The pictures and the sound are checked too, scene by scene, each finding saying what to change: an AI shot that failed (the render would refuse it) or is still being made, a ready shot being generated again or whose last regeneration failed (advice only, said once whatever the number of scenes showing it, naming the take that plays: its previous take plays, in a render too), an animated image whose animation failed or is still being made (advice only: the scene plays the still image), a shot asked with a still camera whose camera moved (`camera moved on a locked-off shot`; a real product screen drawn into a shot follows the camera, so it is not flagged for it; set `motion: intended` on the clip scene when the move is wanted, and a preview of every scene showing the shot that passes clears it, the check naming that preview and its revision), an image smaller than the frame it is drawn on (it would look soft), a screen insert whose corner leaves the frame (advice: the screen keeps its true corners and the insert shows through the visible part - a quad mostly outside the footage the frame shows is a failure; the render and the scene preview also check every frame an insert draws, above), music that ends before the video with `Loop` off, music too quiet to be heard (silent, a long quiet passage, or lost under the voice - advice only, see "Music"), a narration no voice will speak (the voice-over off, or no speech model available), a narration spoken by another voice than the video's because the video's voice failed it (see below), a card or a statement that writes the brand name its logo already spells (`Filigran` in the kicker under the Filigran logo: the audience reads it twice), and an AI shot or a recording still holding its last frame for more than a second and a half under a longer narration (advice: generate a longer shot, continue it with a shot made from its last frame, record more of the flow, or shorten the narration). A shot or a recording shorter than its narration says the speed that makes it last as long, beside `Zoom` to fill the scene, and a screen insert running past the point where a looping shot starts again says where that seam falls and the length that ends the insert before it. When a recording is made, the assistant is told how long it plays at each speed and how much narration that carries, before it writes one. These are read from what the platform knows of your media, so they are shown even where the layout cannot be measured, and the editor reads them again when a shot is generated again, without waiting for the next save.

Before a scene preview or a render starts, everything it would find without drawing a frame - a screen insert off its bezel or on a screen that is not flat, footage shorter than its narration, a frozen last frame, an insert past a loop's seam, text that does not fit or covers other text, a shot or a recording not ready - is checked at once, and the start is refused with the whole list in one answer, each finding saying first when only a new shot fixes it. Corners a little off the bezel are snapped and saved as a new version of the video before the start, said in the answer. Whether a screen is flat is measured once, on the shot itself when the video is saved, so a scene preview and a 4K render give the same answer; a screen that bows a little is no finding when the insert drawn on it still covers it - on its edges, none of the green showing past them - and the save says so as a fact. On a transition's frames drawn smaller than the shot (a 1080p render of 4K footage), a reading of the screen's bezel that disagrees with the save's is set aside for it and said to the administrator in that render's answer, never as a finding of the video.

A recording scene whose kept seconds play its page still loading is flagged at save and refused before any preview or render (`scene_loading_span`): from a click that opened a page or a view to the moment the wait after it saw the page settle, and every span the recording logged a progress indicator or a blank drawing area. A click that stays on its page and sets a drawing moving (a graph laid out again, a legend folded, a view kept in the page address) is not a page loading: its motion is what the scene shows, and only a progress indicator or a blank drawing area it shows counts. The finding names the scene, the recording, the seconds it plays and the fix: move the scene's start past the moment the page settled, cut the scene in two around it, or cut its pauses. Under 0.3 s, a click's own response, it is tolerated.

Two faults that only the whole video would show are refused when the video is saved, before any preview: the same sentence in the narrations of two scenes (each preview hears its own narration only, the whole video would say it twice and fail its sound check), naming both scenes; and a recording on a screen insert that the quality the video is made for - the quality of its last render, 4K before its first - would draw soft, with the window size and the pixel ratio to record it again at, so it is recorded once.

## Templates

A video template keeps every video of a team to the same look. It is a template like the ones that dress presentations - listed under `Agent Studio > Templates`, shared, duplicated and published the same way - and it carries:

- **what it is made for**: product demo, brand film, event, launch, explainer or social - `New video` suggests a template for each of these kinds of video;
- the **palette, fonts, logos and pictures** of the brand (the same colours as the brand's deck);
- the **look it presets**: the backdrop, the watermark, how its videos move (see [Motion design](#motion-design)), the transitions, the captions, the voice-over style, how a statement reads, how a recording sits on screen (the browser window, the cursor, the camera). A preset fills only what the video leaves unset: the editor's `Watermark` field shows the template's corner until you pick another one or `None`, and when a video draws no watermark while its template draws one, saving it says so;
- the **art direction of its AI shots**: the look every AI shot made for the video takes (the light, the lens, the materials, the palette) and what no shot ever shows - see [AI shots wear the template's art direction](#ai-shots-wear-the-templates-art-direction);
- the **cards it adds**: a title card before the first scene and a closing card after the last, when the video has none of its own - a starting point, never locked: a video's own first title card (last closing card), written in the editor or by the assistant, replaces the template's and takes its look;
- the **choices it locks**: a locked choice always takes the template's value, whatever the video asks for; the editor shows it locked, and the assistant is told so when the video is saved.

## Recordings and browser profiles

A recording is captured in the platform's own headless browser - nothing to install - at a high resolution (2400x1350 by default), with human pacing: the cursor glides to each element, typing has a natural rhythm, scrolling is smooth and steady (a scroll step can be given how long it takes, else it moves at the recording's pace; a header pinned while the page scrolls under it, and what the page draws over a graph it zooms, stay exactly as painted on every frame): the wheel turns once a frame of the recording, the same distance each time - 1200 px over 2 seconds is 120 turns of 10 px at 60 fps - and a scroll to the top or the bottom of the page moves it the same way, never by the browser's own smooth scroll. A browser paints a page that large only a few times a second, so while a step scrolls, the recording notes where the page stands 60 times a second and draws the scroll again at 30 frames a second (60 for a video rendered at 60 fps) from what the browser painted: a scroll plays smooth on a screen insert, whatever the size it was recorded at - the page's own scroll or a panel of it that scrolls on its own, a page that repaints slowly included, which scrolls at the pace the wheel turned however late it paints, never repeating a frame it painted late. A scroll over a graph the page draws itself, in 2D or with WebGL - a knowledge graph, a report graph - zooms the graph instead: the wheel then turns at the recording's frame rate, each turn painted before the next, and the zoom is drawn again at that rate from what the browser painted, the toolbars over the graph left still. A hover, a click or typing that fades a tooltip in or opens a menu is drawn again the same way, over the time its animation lasts. A scroll the browser painted at the recording's rate but unevenly - a frame repeated, then one twice as far, the move-move-hold cadence that reads as judder - is drawn again at that rate too. Each scroll keeps how evenly it moves from one frame to the next (the rate it was painted at, the largest, mean and spread of its distance a frame), and a step whose spread exceeds half its mean is said as `stepping motion` in its recording's action log. The assistant is told the frame rate each recording plays at ("scrolls at 30 fps (27 frames a second synthesised between keyframes)"), and each scroll and animation step's own (the rate the browser painted it at, whether it was drawn again, and the panel it scrolled), and a recording whose scrolls or animations play under 24 frames a second is a finding when you save a video that shows it, naming the step ("take 38f88e75 carries 2.1 fps on step 3 (scroll): scrolls will step"): record it again - with the `seconds` it gives for a scroll faster than the page could paint. A frame the browser hands over at another size or scale than the rest of the recording (painted while a graph of the page was read, not what the page showed) is dropped, the frame before held in its place, and the recording's answer lists each run (step, time, frames). Frames that still draw the page at another scale during a wait, a pause, a highlight or a hover, between two frames that are alike, are a capture fault, not a motion of the page: the recording's answer says "off-scale frames at step 24 (pause, 103.0-103.4 s, 0.4 s, 2 frame(s))", and saving a video that shows them is a finding on each scene playing them, naming the frames: record that take again rather than slow it. When a step does not find what it looks for, the assistant is told the closest names the page offered and what the page was filtered by then (a search term a list page kept, the parameters of its address). Each recording keeps its action log (what was clicked, where, when), which is what the camera, the cursor and the callouts are drawn from. Sensitive parts of a page can be blurred in every frame or hidden (a cookie banner). Recordings are made by the assistant from a step script: `Record a new flow with the assistant` in the editor's `Media` tab asks it. A step marked optional (a banner that may not show) gives up after 3 seconds instead of 15, and the time it spent looking is cut from the video, whatever the idle setting. A wait after a click that opens another page or switches a tab holds until the page has loaded - no progress bar or spinner left, the page and its graphs still for half a second - up to 10 seconds past the time it asked for, and the assistant is told when the page settled. A spinner or a placeholder shimmer drawn by an animation that never ends counts as loading too, as long as it moves on screen, even when nothing else on the page changes; an element of the page's own that always animates holds such a wait until its timeout, and the assistant is told so it can turn the wait's settling off. A wait, a pause or a highlight whose frames still show a page loading (a progress bar, a graph not drawn yet) is pointed out to the assistant with the recording ("step 5 holds on a loading page for 2.1 s"), so it records it again before using it, and is noted as advice when you save a video that shows it. When the assistant looks at a page before scripting a recording, it is told how long the page took to settle once it opened, so it gives its waits the time the page needs. A step choosing an option works on any list, not only a classic drop-down: a picker such as a tenant or organisation selector is opened, the option is found by the name it shows (typed first in a picker you search in), and the cursor moves to it and clicks it; the camera frames the picker and its choice. An option the list does not have stops the recording naming the options it offered. A step naming an element by its role and name also finds it under a close role - a menu entry that toggles (`menuitemcheckbox`) for a `menuitem`, a switch for a checkbox, a tree entry for an option, and a menu entry for a button when its name is exact and unique - its own role first; the action log says which role it matched. A recording lasts 5 minutes at most (`VIDEO_STUDIO_MAX_TAKE_SECONDS`): before it starts, its script is timed (its waits, its actions, its typing), and one that would take more than 90% of that is refused, naming the step that crosses the limit and the last marker before it to split the script at. A recording that still reaches the limit - a page slower than expected - is kept up to there, `ready (truncated at step N)`, with its contact sheet. A recording never types a password: a step typing into a password field, or typing one of the profile's stored credentials, is refused (its action log would keep what it typed) - keep the credentials on the browser profile, which signs in by itself. The contact sheet the assistant checks a recording with - and every poster or screen it shows you - is a file of the conversation, so it opens again when you come back to the conversation later.

To record an application you sign in to, use a [browser profile](resources/browser-profiles.md) (`Tools & Integrations > Browser profiles`, or `Browser profiles` at the top of `Work > Videos`): the start URL, the sign-in URL if it differs, the account to use, and the session its sign-in saved. The same profiles let your agents browse the application signed in; their sign-in, MFA, sessions and timeouts are described on that page.

**A recording can act on a graph the page draws itself** (a knowledge graph, an attack path), where nodes and links are not elements a step can name. A click, double-click, right-click, hover, drag or wheel step may act on a point of the page - in pixels of the window, or relative to an element such as the graph (`0.5, 0.5` is its centre, so the point holds whatever the window's size) - or on a node by its label. Nothing is needed from the application filmed - any release, any instance, a third-party tool: the recorder finds the node's entry in the list a graph keeps for screen readers (one entry per node, invisible on screen), reads the label painted under the node on the graph, and when it cannot read it (labels overlapping, a dense cluster, a graph zoomed out too far to show labels) makes that entry the active one, as a keyboard user does, and takes the node the graph highlights. It does so again before every step, so a node moved by a pan, a zoom or a new layout is followed, and the recording's log says how each node was found and where the cursor acted on it - for a recording that stopped too. A label drawn on a link (along it, turned with it or in italic) and a card that repeats the node's name over the graph are never taken for the node's label. The cursor glides to the node and rests on it a quarter of a second, as a person hovering before a right-click does, then clicks; a right-click notes the menu it opened and checks it is the node's - the graph heads it with the node's name - so a right-click beside the node stops the recording right there, saying what the menu was headed with and where the cursor was, with a picture of the graph marking that point in the contact sheet; a drag moves smoothly to where it releases (a lasso, a node moved, another node), a wheel zooms, and keys can be held meanwhile (`Control` + click adds to a selection). The camera zooms on a node clicked as it does on a button. A label the graph's list does not hold stops the recording naming its three closest entries; a node neither painted nor highlighted on the graph stops it too, saying which, with a picture of the graph in the contact sheet. A step on an entry of the list a graph keeps for screen readers only stops the recording at that step, naming the node step to use instead: a click there would land beside the graph.

**Animations are captured at the recording's frame rate.** Before the first step, the recording measures how fast this server can capture frames of its size. When it cannot keep up with the recording's frame rate, the recording is made at pixel ratio 1.5 if that keeps up (the assistant is told). Otherwise it is refused, naming the largest window and pixel ratio this server was measured to keep up at (measured then, or remembered from the recordings made on it in the last 15 minutes) and, for a 60 fps recording, 30 fps at the size asked when that keeps up. The assistant can ask for either fallback in advance, so the recording is made on it instead of being refused, and the recording says which it used. When the server is busy with other work - scene previews, a render or another recording - and that is what keeps it from keeping up, the recording is not refused: it waits in the recording lane of the render queue, and starts by itself once that work has eased enough to keep up. Meanwhile the assistant says its place in the lane, the recordings ahead of it and what it waits for ("busy: 2 scene previews and 1 render on its worker"); once made, the recording says how long it waited. A recording is refused only when the server itself cannot keep up with nothing else running, or after waiting six hours; that refusal names the work that was still running, and asking again queues it once more. A recording whose server was stopped under it (a deploy, a restart) is not charged a retry for it: one whose steps were done is finished from the frames it captured, its steps not played again, and says so; one started again from its script on a server that cannot yet keep up with its frame rate waits up to 30 minutes in the recording lane for one that can, instead of being refused. Scene previews always give way to a recording, never the reverse. A graph the page draws itself, in 2D or with WebGL, is drawn on the server's GPU when it has one, and by the browser's software GPU otherwise, so it animates on a server without one too, more slowly: the recording says which (GPU GL or software GL). The recording keeps how many frames it captured each second and each step's rate, and a stretch where the page animated at under 20 frames a second for more than a second is said when it is made and when you save a video showing it (`low capture rate at step 4 (12 fps for 2.3 s)`): record it again. Each such graph is also measured on its own: every transition it plays (a graph laid out again) is listed at its step in the recording's action log, naming the graph's element, how long it moved and the rate it was captured at, and one under 20 frames a second is said the same way, however short (`low capture rate at step 4`, with the graph's element, its rate, how long it moved and software GL). Drawn by the software GPU, it is recorded faster only on a server with a GPU: your administrator can add one (see the deployment's "Sizing the workers"). A recording scene played slowed down (speed under 1) over such a stretch blends the frames on either side instead of holding each one longer. On a server without a GPU, a 60 fps recording of a graph steps on its transitions: the recording says so when it is made and offers 30 fps, which halves the frames each transition has to fill. When you save, each such transition is said on every scene that shows it, in one check: a brief one painted fast enough to still read as motion is advice, a longer one or one that holds a frame on screen for more than a quarter of a second is a finding the render waits for. The finding offers slowing the scene only where the recording kept the frames to blend there.

**The assistant looks before it records**: it can open a page of the signed-in application in the profile's saved session, without typing anything or running a step, and read its screenshot and the names of what can be clicked or typed in - the names the menu really shows ("Agentic Flow", not "Flows") - so the recording's steps find their elements the first time. It looks at the page in the same clean state a recording starts in (below), so the names it reads are the names the recording finds. A step that still misses its element is reported with the three closest names the page offered at that moment, the page's address and title, and how many elements of that kind it showed; when one of your recent recordings with the same profile found that element, the report says which one and at which address ("the page is in another state"). The contact sheet keeps the frame where the step failed, also for a recording the failure stopped.

**Every recording starts from a clean page state.** An application often remembers how you left a page: a graph's layout, a list's filters, kept in the browser's storage and sometimes written into the page's address. A browser profile's saved session would carry that from one recording to the next, so a recording that changed a graph's layout would leave the next one on a page its steps were not written for. Once the session is checked, each recording opens its start page exactly as given, after clearing what the application kept in the browser's storage for that site. Cookies are never cleared, and neither are the storage keys the profile lists as part of its sign-in (`keep_storage_keys`, on the [browser profile](resources/browser-profiles.md)). The first line of the recording's action log says what was cleared and the address the recording opened on. The assistant can keep the page state as the session carried it, choose what is cleared, or clear it again before a step that opens a page. When clearing the storage signs the application out, the recording stops and says so, naming the keys it cleared: list the sign-in keys on the profile.

**Recordings start signed in, or not at all**: every recording checks the session before its first step. When it has ended, a profile that can sign in by itself (its password and TOTP secret, or the identity provider's saved session) signs in again first; otherwise the recording stops with `profile not authenticated: landed on <page>` and makes no video - sign the profile in, then record again. Some applications show their sign-in page for a moment while they read the session, then open: the recording waits for the application to show signed in (the element or the address you set on the profile, else the same check as the session check) and starts only then, so its first frame is the application, never its sign-in page. An application that does not open signed in within the profile's sign-in timeout stops the recording the same way. A recording may use any profile you may use; the profile does not need to be given to the assistant.

Recordings reach public addresses only, unless an administrator allows [browser profiles on private networks](resources/browser-profiles.md#private-networks) and the profile declares the hosts.

**Screen inserts are checked on every frame**: a render reads every frame of each screen insert it draws - a stroke of the inserted page seen through the person in front, a ghost of the mask, a halo, a frame off the bezel - not only its first, middle and last frames, and fails on a defect that lasts a few frames. Its quality report says, per insert, how many of its frames were read (`every frame` or `sampled`) and when its worst frame is. To look at an insert whole, ask the assistant for an insert sweep: one picture per scene, the insert's region at 100 % every second (or the step you ask), each moment labelled with its time and the failing ones framed in red.

## AI shots, voice-over and music

- **AI shots** are animated footage generated by a video model - an opening shot, b-roll behind a statement, a place or people at work that sets the scene - optionally starting from a picture or from a frame of your own footage, so the shot opens on something real. That frame can be an image, a step of a recording (the frame its contact sheet shows after that step), a moment of an uploaded or rendered video, or the last frame of a recording or of another shot, which the new shot then continues: placed right after that footage, it carries on from where the footage stops. Ask the assistant for it ("start a shot from the dashboard step of my recording"). Before it pays for the shot, the assistant looks at that frame and moves it if it is not the right picture; a page caught still loading (a spinner, a blank page) is noticed, and the shot starts once the page has settled instead. The frame taken from your footage is kept as a file of the conversation: the assistant shows it with the finished shot, the inspector shows it under `First frame` with where it was taken, and a shot generated again starts from that same picture, even once the recording is gone. A frame of your footage must have the shot's shape: a landscape recording does not start a vertical shot unless the assistant is asked to crop it, which keeps the middle of the frame at the shot's shape (a picture is used as it is). They are scenes of a video like recordings: generate one from the editor's `Media` tab (or ask the assistant) and it joins the video at once, shown as being generated until it is ready. They use the video model your administrator chose under `Settings > AI Models > Video Generation` (see [Video generation](../administration/settings.md#video-generation)): Veo on Google Gemini, or what an OpenAI-compatible gateway such as LiteLLM serves (Veo on Vertex AI, Runway); the editor lets you pick another listed model for one shot. A model named for a shot, or for a shot generated again, is made by the provider that lists it - never by the one chosen in Settings - and a model two of your providers offer is made by the one your video's shots of that model are made on (a shot generated again: the one it was made on), else by the one chosen in Settings, and the shot says which - you say which only when neither of them offers it. A model its provider has retired is never offered or used - OpenAI retired its Sora video models on 2026-09-24 - and when no video model is available at all, the assistant says why before it plans the video, and nothing is generated to fail. When a model does not make the size, length or resolution asked, the shot is made at one it does and the shot says so; a provider that is busy or briefly unreachable (its load, an outage, no answer) never fails the shot nor hands it to another model: the shot waits in the queue for that very model and is asked again after 30 s, then 1, 2, 5 and 10 minutes, then every 10 minutes, for up to a day - the assistant and the inspector say `provider busy, next try at 17:45, 3 tries`. A provider's rate limit or quota never fails a shot on the spot (see below). A shot is never paid for twice: a generation interrupted by a restart of the platform is picked up where the provider is, and a video already made is kept even if what follows it fails. The platform generates a few shots at a time (four by default, `VIDEO_STUDIO_CLIP_CONCURRENCY`), so when the assistant asks for more at once, the others wait their turn: the assistant waits for each shot for a few minutes at most (`VIDEO_STUDIO_CLIP_INLINE_WAIT_SECONDS`), and a shot still being made after that goes on in the background. The assistant is told why - waiting for a free slot, queued at the provider, rate limited - and how far the shot is, and checks on it before rendering.
- **The same subject in every shot**: a shot can be given one to three reference pictures - a character, an animal, a garment, a product, a logo - that the video model keeps the same, wherever it stands in the frame: give every shot of a film the same references and the subject no longer changes from shot to shot. A start frame is for continuity (the shot carries on from a picture), reference pictures for consistency. Only some video models take them (Veo 3.1 and Veo 3.1 Fast, without a start frame and at 8 seconds; Kling 3.0 Omni, beside a start frame; Seedance 2 on Runway, without a start frame); the assistant is told which, and a shot asked with references on a model that takes none is refused before anything is paid. A shot generated again keeps its references. A shot is asked in one of three modes - a start frame only, reference pictures only, or both on the models that take them together - and the assistant is told which each time. Generating a shot again switches between them: giving it references on a model that takes one or the other drops its start frame by itself (`Start frame dropped`); the assistant can also drop the start frame on purpose (`start_frame: false`), go back to a start frame alone, which drops the references (`start_frame: true` with the picture to start from: `Reference images dropped`), or start the shot from another picture or another moment of its footage. The picture it started from stays in the conversation. Before generating many shots at once, the assistant can check them all first (`validate_only`): every check runs - the model, the references, the start frame and its shape - and the answer says the mode and whether the provider cools; nothing is generated or paid; a combination the model cannot take is refused at once, naming the model and the two ways out.
- **Ingredients - your logo, your product, a character**: in place of bare reference pictures, a shot can be given one to three ingredients, each with what it is and, if you like, its name ("the Filigran logo"), and it keeps the picture it starts from on any video model. A model that takes reference pictures beside that start receives them; any other model is told to keep each one exactly as in the picture the shot starts from, so that picture must show them - the assistant composes it first when needed. On such a model a **logo** is also drawn back onto the finished shot with its own pixels: it is found in the picture the shot starts from (or placed where the assistant says), followed through every frame as the camera moves, and drawn over what the model made of it, so it is never misspelt. It is drawn in exactly one place in every frame: on an object that moves past something else (a mug falling over a table), the surface carrying the logo is followed, never what lies behind it. When the object turns its logo away from the camera (a mug that rolls), the logo fades out over three frames instead of being squeezed into a smear, is not drawn while it faces away, and fades back in where it faces the camera again; an object at rest keeps its logo perfectly still. A shot that loses sight of a logo facing the camera (the camera cuts away, a hand covers it) fails as `logo lost`, its video kept to look at. Every finished shot is checked for its logo showing twice in a frame: such a shot says `logo duplicated at t=...` with that frame, and a scene preview or a render of a scene playing it fails until the shot is generated again. The shot says which way its ingredients went (sent, named, composited or tracked), and a shot generated again keeps them.
- **A logo on a subject the shot does not open on.** An establishing shot of a building or a street, with the branded subject walking in later, has no logo in the picture it starts from. Give the logo its subject instead: a picture of the subject wearing the logo (a conversation file, or a frame saved from another shot) and, if you like, what to call it ("the cow in the navy blanket"). Once the shot is made, the subject is found in its frames, the surface the logo sits on - the blanket, the garment, the panel - is followed through every frame, and the logo is drawn there with its own pixels, in perspective and under the light of the scene, on every instance of the subject (a herd each wearing it gets one logo each), from the frame it enters to the frame it leaves. This works on every video model, with or without a start picture; a model that takes reference pictures is sent the logo first, and the logo is drawn onto the subject only when that take does not show it as given. A shot whose subject never appears fails as `ingredient subject not found`; one whose surface cannot be followed past a frame (a hand over it, a fast turn) fails as `ingredient surface lost at t=...`, that frame kept to look at. The shot says the share of its frames the logo was drawn on. Ask the assistant ("the cow wears our logo on its blanket - it walks into the street shot").
- **Every shot's frames are kept and measured**: once a shot is made, its first, middle and last frames are kept with it - the assistant sees them with the shot, the inspector shows them under `The shot's frames` - with how close its first frame stayed to the picture it started from, the share of that picture's words it still reads, and how far its last frame moved from its first. Ask for a minimum likeness ("keep the first frame close to the screenshot") and a take that drifted further from its picture fails as `drifted from reference`, its video kept for you to see, to be generated again in place.
- **The same prop or character in every scene**: a recurring prop - the branded rug the hero wears, a product, a mascot - tends to drift from one AI shot to the next (a collar appears, the piping changes colour) while every shot passes its own checks, because each is read against its own prompt and start picture only. Give the video `references`: a picture of the prop or character as it should look (a crop saved from the scene that got it right, with `save_video_frame`, or a portrait), with a name and, if you like, a few words on what must stay the same. Then name the references each shot's scene must match (`consistency`). When the video is saved, the first, middle and last frames of every such shot are read against each reference - the prop is found in the frame, its structure and its dominant colours compared; a change of light is not a drift - and a scene whose shot drifted is flagged `reference drift: the branded rug differs from the video's reference` with the frame and the crop it was read on, to generate the shot again in place. A shot generated with `consistency` is held to the references as it is made: on a video model that takes reference pictures beside a start frame, the reference's picture is sent along; on any other, the prompt names it ("keep the branded rug exactly as in the start picture: navy quilted rug, teal piping"); the assistant is told which, and a take that drifted fails before it is accepted, its video kept to look at. A shot naming no reference is checked as before. A reference the video does not have is refused by name when the spec is saved.
- **The picture a shot starts from** can be any picture: a PNG or a JPEG goes as it is, an SVG (a logo, a badge, a diagram of a template) is drawn first, and any other picture (WebP, GIF...) is converted - every video model receives a picture it reads. One that is no picture at all is refused when the shot is asked for, before anything is paid.
- **The text of a shot is checked.** A video model asked to move a diagram, a badge or a screenshot redraws its labels and sometimes garbles them ("Orcheatration"). Once a shot made from a picture is generated, the words of its first and last frames are read and compared with the picture's - with the words the picture itself reads clearly, that is: lettering that is small, soft or at an angle in the start picture (flags on poles, a distant banner, signage seen edge-on) reads as garbage there and as other garbage on the shot, so it is recorded as unreadable in the start picture and never held against the shot. The shot says which words were checked, which were left out and how surely they read, and - reported, never a failure - any lettering it shows that the picture does not, which is what a drifting model usually adds. When a word is drawn wrong, the picture's own lettering is first drawn back onto every frame of the shot, following the camera, and the shot is read again - once, with nothing new generated or billed. Only a shot that still reads wrong fails as `text drift`, naming the word, the frame and a crop of it, so it can be generated again (a calmer camera move helps) or the scene plays the picture as a still. Small text the model melts into unreadable marks (a screen in a photo, a badge) fails it the same way, and the failure names each place: its frame and time, its box, what the picture reads there and a crop of it, telling the picture's own lettering redrawn (keep that region from the picture, or blur it) from new marks the model drew where the picture had no lettering (simplify the object that carries them, or name such marks in the negative prompt). Before a shot is paid for, a validation answers the picture's text regions the check will hold the shot to, so they can be blanked or kept first. A word the camera moved out of frame is not a drift. An animated image whose animation fails plays as its still image. The reading is done by your chat model; when it cannot run, the shot is kept and says its text was not checked. Lettering the shot invents where the picture had none - a pill, a subtitle in letters that make no word, no brand name and no number - is reported with a crop of it, and the video's quality check refuses a render on it until the shot is generated again or that region is kept from the picture; a picture already carrying such lettering is refused before the shot is paid for, naming where. The prompt sent tells the model that every printed word of the picture stays exactly as it is. Ask the assistant to skip the check for a shot meant to change the picture's words.
- **What a shot shows is checked against its prompt.** A shot whose prompt names subjects ("six cows grazing in a meadow") has its first, middle and last frames read once it is made: a subject missing, of another kind, deformed (extra limbs, human hands on an animal), turned into something else, or something the shot was asked not to show, fails the shot as `content drift`, naming what came out - its video is kept for you to see, and it can be generated again in place. A subject described as several variants ("five cats, each a different colour: ginger, grey, white, black, tabby") is checked as several distinct cats, none identical: two identical cats fail, a variant that does not show never does. A shot started from a picture is checked on that picture before anything is paid, the check of a draft included: when the picture itself already fails the check (two look-alike cats where the prompt asks for two different ones, a subject of another kind), every take would open on it and fail the same way, so the shot is refused (`start_fails_content_check`) with what the picture shows, and the remedies: another start picture, reference pictures of the subjects instead, or no check for a shot meant to transform the scene. A take that fails on its first frame for something its start picture shows says so: inherited from the start picture, not drift of the model. A subject that walks out of the frame for a moment is not a drift, and a shot whose prompt names no subject (a landscape, an abstract motion) is read only for what it was asked not to show and for what its template avoids (said, never failed). A detail nobody notices at full HD - a marker that lost its dot, a seam on a sleeve - is minor drift: the shot is kept and its notes name the detail; ask the assistant for a strict check on a close-up where every detail shows. The reading is done by your chat model; when it cannot run, the shot is kept and says it was not checked. Ask the assistant to skip the check for a shot meant to transform its subject. When two takes in a row show the same thing the shot was asked not to show (a coloured light the model keeps drawing), the next take asks for it in the prompt itself - "No red light anywhere in the shot." - rather than in the negative prompt alone, and turns the model's guidance down where the model has such a setting; the answer says so, and the take is still checked against what the shot was asked not to show.
- **Screens a shot was asked not to show.** A video model asked for an office "without screens or readable text" sometimes draws monitors showing an interface anyway. Instead of failing such a shot, the assistant can ask for those screens to be cleaned: flattened (switched off, keeping the room's soft light on them), or showing a real screenshot you choose. Only a shot whose one problem is what those screens show is cleaned; each screen is followed through every frame, as the camera moves, and drawn over. A screen someone passes in front of is never drawn over (the shot fails as before, saying why), and the cleaned shot is read again: anything else it still shows that it was asked not to fails it.
- **The screen of a shot a screen insert goes on is kept clear.** A video model told not to draw a head, a hand or an animal in front of the screen sometimes draws it anyway. A shot carrying a screen insert - a real product screen, a shot asked as footage an insert goes on, or one whose start picture shows the screen a scene draws an insert on - is asked, at the end of its prompt, that nothing enters the screen's rectangle ("nothing enters the rectangle x 0.30-0.70, y 0.20-0.60 of the frame"), and a video model that takes reference pictures beside its start picture is sent the start picture a second time as one; the answer says which. Once the take is made, what stands in front of the screen is measured on every frame, as the insert's matte will see it, and the answer gives its largest share of the screen and the frame it reaches it on. Something the shot was asked not to show seen only in front of the screen is judged by that measure rather than failing the shot outright: under 10% of the screen, with the screen showing a green, blue or magenta key to matte it by and the mask over 0.7 sure, the shot is kept, the insert drawn under it, and its notes say so; above that it fails as `content drift`, the measure in the reason. Every `content drift` failure also says which attempt the take was and how many of the 5 regenerations of its prompt and start picture remain.
- **A real product screen in an AI shot.** To film the product on a screen - a laptop on a desk at dusk, a booth wall, a control room - the shot starts from a picture of the room with the screen in it, and the real screenshot is named with the screen's four corners in that picture. When the screen of that picture is one solid green, blue or magenta surface (ask the assistant to draw the room that way), its corners are found in the picture rather than guessed, and the assistant can check them before anything is paid. The video model draws the room, the light and the reflections around a flat screen, and the screenshot is drawn into that screen in every frame, following the camera, under anything that passes in front of it: its text is exact, so it is never checked for text drift. The screenshot is kept whole, letterboxed in its own background colour where the screen has another shape (a sidebar or a right column is never cut; asking to fill the screen instead is refused when it would cut the content), and it is carried to the screen's bezel, so no line of green stays between it and the frame around it. A shot whose model did not keep the screen flat (a fast camera, the screen leaving the frame) fails as `screen lost`, its video kept to look at, and is generated again with a calmer move; so does one that leaves green around the screen (`key spill`) or whose screen edge jitters from frame to frame (`key flicker`). A camera move the model adds anyway is not a failure while the screen is followed. A camera move is measured on the shot's frames, never guessed from a still picture: to hold a shot to a still camera, ask the assistant for a camera tolerance, and a take whose camera moves more fails as `camera moved`, its video kept. Some video models do not hold a locked-off camera whatever the prompt says (Veo from a prompt alone drifts, from a start frame it holds; Kling holds it): with a tight tolerance (0.5 or less), a new shot is made with a model that holds it, and when you named the model, the assistant says before anything is paid that it will not hold the camera, and what would. Ask the assistant ("show our dashboard on a laptop in a dim office").
- **A drifting camera is locked off.** A video model asked for a still camera often lets it drift a little - a slow push, a few pixels of pan. When a shot is asked with a camera tolerance, or its prompt asks for a still camera, the finished take is stabilised before it is measured: every frame is locked to the first one and the edges the drift uncovers are cropped away, so a take that only drifted is kept instead of failed. The stabilised take is kept only when it really moves less; otherwise - the camera could not be followed, the drift is too large to crop (more than a fifth of the frame), or locking it would not help - the take stays as the model made it and says why. The un-stabilised take is kept beside the stabilised one, and the inspector says how much the motion went down (`Stabilised: motion 0.42 to 0.03`). A shot with your real screen drawn into it is never cropped into: when no crop keeps that screen whole, the shot stays as the model made it and says so. The assistant can also ask for it on any shot, or turn it off for one.
- **Another model when a take fails - only when you ask.** A shot is made on its video's model and never on another by itself: no fallback is set by default, and a shot generated again keeps its model unless you name another. When you ask for it on a shot, the assistant can give that shot one to three other video models to fall back to, for that request only. When a take fails a check of its frames - its camera moved, it drifted from its start picture, its text or its content drifted, its screen or its logo was lost - the shot is generated again in place with the next model, without waiting to be asked; each model is tried once, and each is checked before anything is paid (one that cannot take the shot's reference pictures is refused, and so is one its provider no longer serves - it answered "not found" for it - named in the answer). A model a provider lists but no longer serves is left out of the models the assistant offers, with the reason, from the moment the provider says so. The shot's own model named among them is simply left out, the assistant saying so - its first take is already made with it. The shot then says which model made the take it kept, and why the others failed (`Made by the fallback model kling-v3` in the inspector).
- **Silent shots**: some video models make their own sound (8 seconds of synthetic ambience for a Veo shot). The assistant can ask a shot silent - the model is asked for no sound, and any sound it makes anyway is removed before the shot is stored - which is what a shot under a music bed or a narration wants; the shots that animate a picture are always silent.
- **A provider's rate limit** never fails a shot on the spot: a shot the provider refuses because too many were asked at once (its limit per minute) waits and is asked again - after the wait the provider names, else after a short pause - for up to 30 minutes, nothing being made or billed meanwhile, and the assistant says it is rate limited. Past that, it fails saying so. The refusal is the whole batch's, not the shot's: that provider cools until the wait it asked for (or a wait that grows each time it refuses again after a cooling ended - the shots of a batch refused together count as one refusal), and every other shot asked of it meanwhile waits in the queue without being asked - saying until when (`Waiting in the queue until ...` in the inspector) - and starts by itself once it cools. A quota (the day's, the plan's) is usually counted per model: a refusal for a model's quota cools that model alone, and the provider's other models still take shots. A shot refused for a quota waits up to a day, even when the provider asks for the whole day: a quota is never a failed shot, nor a shot moved to another provider. A quota refusal that names no wait cools its model for an hour. Where the provider says how many credits the account has left (ElevenLabs), a shot costing more is refused before anything is paid or queued, saying both (`quota: 3448 remaining, 7272 needed`). The assistant is told before it queues a batch (`validate_only` and the list of video models say which model cools, and until when), so it queues the batch once or picks a model that does not cool.
- **A shot that fails** says why where it plays - the preview, the timeline, the inspector (a prompt the provider's safety system blocked, a model the provider does not serve, a refused key, a text drift). The video keeps saving and previewing around it. `Generate again` (in the inspector, with the prompt you can reword) makes it again in place: every video using it keeps it. The assistant can also generate again a shot that is ready but came out wrong: the shot keeps playing its take - in the preview, the quality check and a render - until the new take is ready and has passed its checks, then the new take replaces it in every video using it. A new take that fails (refused, drifted, cancelled) leaves the previous one playing and nothing broken: the inspector says `The last regeneration failed: the previous take still plays` with the reason, the quality check says it as advice, and the failed attempt's frames are kept to look at until a new take replaces it. Nothing already made is generated again to get the previous take back. The assistant can drop the previous take at once instead, when you want it gone whatever happens (`discard`). A failed shot is never kept as media: it is not listed, and once no video uses it, it is deleted an hour after it failed. Each attempt is a new generation billed by the provider, so one shot is generated again at most five times from the same prompt and start picture; a reworded prompt or another start picture starts the count again, and a retry that only asks another model or a different camera tolerance is never counted. The assistant keeps fixing a shot that failed - a reworded prompt, another model - and stops only when the very same request keeps failing. A video renders once all its shots are ready.
- **Voice-over** is spoken from each scene's narration by the speech model your administrator chose under `Settings > AI Models > Speech Generation` (see [Speech generation](../administration/settings.md#speech-generation)): OpenAI, Azure OpenAI, Gemini, ElevenLabs - with your team's cloned brand voice - or what an OpenAI-compatible gateway such as LiteLLM serves. A video, or its template, can name another speech model and voice (`Video > Voice-over` in the editor), and a delivery style for the models that take one. A video that follows the platform's speech model and has a style is spoken by a model of the same provider that follows the style when the platform's does not (OpenAI's `tts-1` and `tts-1-hd` take no style, `gpt-4o-mini-tts` does); a video naming a model that ignores its style is told so when it is saved, not after the render. `Speech model` says which model the platform uses when the video names none; a video that names its own keeps it when the platform's changes, and the assistant says which one will speak each time it saves the video. Each scene is spoken knowing the lines before and after it, so a long narration stays continuous on the models that support it. The editor's preview speaks the narration with the same voice, and the render reuses it; both speak as you, through the speech models you may use. Without a speech model available to you, the narration is shown as captions only, and the render says so.
- **Pronunciations** tell the voice how to say a word it would misread: a product name, an acronym. Your administrator keeps the platform's pronunciation lexicon under `Settings > AI Models > Pronunciation lexicon` (every save is a new version; an older one can be restored), and a video adds its own under `Video > Voice-over > Pronunciations`, an entry of the video replacing the platform's for the same written form. An entry has the term as written, how it is said (letters read one by one as capitals joined by hyphens: `X-T-M`), and optionally its IPA, how it is matched (the whole word in the same case, the whole token only, or any case) and what reads it (the voice, the captions or both: the captions keep the written words otherwise). The `Play` button beside each term speaks it with the video's voice, so you can choose a voice on how it says your brand before rendering. Each speech model gets the term the strongest way it can take: its phonetic spelling when it reads one (OpenAI's newer voices, such as `gpt-4o-mini-tts`, take the IPA in their delivery instruction), the provider's own pronunciation dictionary, or the narration rewritten; letters read one by one are always sent as one word - one phonetic token for the letters together (`XTM One` as `XTM` said in one breath, then `One`), or the letters written together - never as letters separated by hyphens or spaces, which a voice reads with a pause after each; when the model can take no IPA (`tts-1-hd`), the term is said by how the entry says it, and the render's notes say so. Then every narration is heard back: the speech-to-text must write the term as written (case, punctuation and spaces aside: `Open CTI` is `OpenCTI`), as the entry says it, or in words that sound like its IPA with at most one sound off. A term the voice says otherwise (`CTEM` heard as `stem`, `Filigran` heard as `Phil Igram`) fails it even when every other word is right, and so does a term not said as one word - a pause inside it of more than about a sixth of a second (`Phil... egran`, `Open... C-T-I`, letters spelled one at a time) fails it as spelled with pauses, whatever speech model said it; a term the lexicon reads as a word that the voice spells letter by letter, even without a pause (`CTEM` said `C-T-E-M` where its entry says `see-tem`), fails it as read letter by letter: the speech-to-text writes `CTEM` for both, so the voice's own syllables are counted (four where `see-tem` has two); a number counts as its words whether it is written in digits or in words, by the speech-to-text or in the entry (`XTM 1` is `XTM One`, `85%` is `eighty-five percent`, an entry said `Windows 11` heard as `Windows eleven`). A narration that fails is spoken again (the same voice with the letters of each term written as one word; a term heard wrong twice is then said differently, by how its entry says it and set apart by commas - `Run the drills with, Open AEV, every week.` - which the render's notes say; then slower; then the closest voice of the same speech model) - never placed silently. The finished video's voice is heard once more, after its encoding; the render's checks say, for each term, how often it is said and how often as the lexicon says it in the takes kept, and every take that failed on it before being replaced (`OpenAEV 3 of 3 in the takes kept, 1 attempt failed on scene st-demo (heard 'OpenA')`), so a term that needed a retry never reads as passed every time; a term accepted in other words than its entry writes (`Filigran` heard as `Philigran`) is a warning with its time, so you can listen to that spot without the render failing. When a video is saved, the quality check warns about a narration that writes a term in another case than its entry (`Opencti`), unless the entry only changes the case of a word said as written (`eXtended` said `extended`), a term the lexicon reads as a word spelled out letter by letter (`C-T-E-M`), a brand term the brand owner gave a reading for that the video's lexicon has no entry for (`CTEM has no pronunciation; the voice may spell it`, with the brand's reading to add), and a brand-like word no entry covers. These warnings are about the narration only: a title or a line on screen is never checked against the lexicon. A template can carry the brand's own list.
- **The voice is mastered**: the silence speech engines leave before and after each line is trimmed, the rumble removed, the sibilants softened, the level evened, and every scene and every speech provider comes out at the same loudness.

### AI shots wear the template's art direction

An AI shot is made for a video, and takes the art direction of the template that video wears, whoever asks for it - you in the editor, the assistant, the animation of a picture. The platform adds it to the shot itself, so a prompt only says what the shot shows (the subject, the action, the camera):

- **The look** is added to the prompt of a shot drawn from a description. A shot that starts from a picture or from your footage keeps that picture's look.
- **What no shot shows** is added to the negative prompt of a video model that takes one (it is never written into a prompt: a model draws what its prompt names, so a model without a negative prompt is steered by the look alone). The finished shot's frames are read for it: a shot that shows it anyway is kept - a generated shot is paid for - and says so, and you or the assistant can generate it again in place if it stands out.
- **A shot made under another template's direction** (made for another video, say) is named when the video is saved, as advice: it keeps playing, and generating it again from the inspector (`Generate again`) or by the assistant makes it under this video's art direction, in place. Shots made before templates had an art direction, free-style shots and shots started from a picture are left as they are.

The art direction never limits how many AI shots a video has, and never stops a shot or a render.

A shot asked for no video at all takes the platform's default template's direction; a free-style video's shots take none. A template's direction is shown on its page and in `New video` (`Directs its AI shots`, with what it never shows), and lives in its rules (`extra_metadata.video.shots`, see [Templates](resources/templates.md#video-templates)): duplicate a built-in template to write your own.

### Music

Music is your choice: the assistant adds it only when you ask for it, or proposes it in its first answer with "no music" as an equal option, and follows your reply. The same goes for the narration, the captions, the length and the pace: whatever the brief leaves open, its first answer says what it will do and the alternatives.

Every video can have a music bed, chosen in `Video > Music and sound` (or by the assistant) from three sources:

- **Library**: royalty-free tracks the platform composes for each video - nothing to license, no provider needed. A track is written for the video it plays under: its length, its sections changing on the scene changes, its final chord on the last frame; it plays sparser under a narration and lifts on a statement or an AI shot without one. Seven moods: Bright Horizon (uplifting, launches and demos), Deep Focus (calm, narrated walkthroughs), Signal (dark, threat briefings), Ascent (cinematic, brand films), Late Notes (lo-fi, social clips), Momentum (energetic, teasers and event recaps) and Quiet Light (minimal piano, customer stories). `Play` beside each one plays half a minute of it.
- **AI music**: describe the music (genre, mood, instruments, tempo, how it builds) and `Compose for this video`: a music model composes it at the video's length, instrumental, with the model your administrator chose under `Settings > AI Models > Music Generation` (see [Music generation](../administration/settings.md#music-generation)). It takes up to a few minutes; the preview plays it once it is ready, and a track that failed says why and can be composed again in place. Your AI tracks can be reused in your other videos. Never name an artist or a song: the providers refuse it. Compose it once the shots are made: a video grows as its shots come out and its narration is spoken, and a track composed early can end before it. When it does, the assistant is told when the video is saved ("Music ends at 142 s of 183 s"; for an AI track that would loop more than 2 seconds before the end, the save's quality check warns "track 144 s under a 146.5 s video: refit or accept the loop"), and can compose the same track again at the video's new length, in place; the render's notes say how long the track is against the video. The save measures the video as its render will play it: each narration at the length it was spoken in the video's voice - the template's opening and closing cards included -, the cuts on the music's beats when they land on them, and, once the video is rendered, the render's own length when it came out longer; a narration not spoken yet counts at the reading pace, and the save says how many were ("about 175 s (every narration at its spoken length)"). So a track that fits the save fits the render. When you like the track as it is, the assistant can extend it instead of replacing it: the take you approved is kept exactly as it plays, and only its ending is composed again, as its continuation, to the video's length. This needs a music model that continues a clip (Eleven Music's `music_v2` and `music_v2_5`, an ElevenLabs enterprise feature; Lyria composes no continuation); when the take's own model cannot, the extension is composed with one that can - one of the take's own provider first, another provider's only when it has none -, on that provider's account, and the assistant names it so you can refuse (the take then keeps playing as it was). An account with no credits left refuses the composition before anything is queued, and if the extension cannot be made, the take keeps playing as it was and the assistant says why at once. A take only a few seconds short of its video needs no model at all: the assistant can pad it instead, keeping the take exactly as it plays up to its last chord, holding that chord to the video's length and fading it out on the last frame - no model call, no credits, at most 8 seconds past the take (held longer, a chord sounds like a drone). When a track set to play once ends less than 3 seconds before the video, the save proposes the pad first; an extension refused for want of a model or of credits proposes it too. A track that plays once and ends before the video is also met halfway by the template's opening and closing cards: one whose length you left unset is shortened to the least its words take to read - the closing card first, the opening card only when that is not enough -, and the save says which card it shortened, from what to what; the length it set is the card's from then on, yours to change. A track is composed 8 seconds longer than the video (and never shorter when it is composed again) - than its current render when that came out longer than planned, the voice taking more time than estimated - so its own ending lands after the closing card and the music fades out on the last frame. Every save says whether the video's current render was made with the music it plays now: after the track is composed again, the render still plays the earlier take until the video is rendered again.
- **Upload**: any sound file of yours.

Whatever the source, the music is levelled against the voice: `Volume` is its level where no one speaks (1 is as loud as the voice), and it goes down under every sentence (`Lower it under the voice`, by 10 dB by default) a moment before the first word, and comes back up after the last - never pumping in between. A clip that keeps its own sound lowers it too. An uploaded or AI track shorter than the video repeats with a crossfade; with `Loop` off it plays once, fading out on its own end, and the rest of the video plays without music - it is never repeated, and the save says how much of the end that leaves silent. `Start at` skips its intro, and `The track's own end` lets its ending land on the video's end. With the music fading out at the end, the quiet seconds where the track dies away under the closing card are expected: the render's check says so ("the track decays under the closing card, by design") rather than warning about a quiet passage. The editor's preview plays the music as the render will mix it.

How loud the music really plays is measured, whatever its source: after each save (as part of the quality check) and again on the render. Three numbers are given, on the scale of the finished video: its loudness where no one speaks, its loudness under the voice once lowered and how far below the voice that is, and its longest stretch quieter than -35 LUFS. A track that is silent or too quiet to hear, a quiet passage of six seconds or more, or music lost under the voice (more than 28 dB below it) is reported with what to change - raise `Volume`, lower it less under the voice, start the track elsewhere or choose another one. Music that competes with the voice (less than 6 dB below it while someone speaks) is reported too, with the opposite advice. It is advice: it never stops a render.

Each render and scene preview also measures its finished sound as a viewer hears it. Per scene and for the whole video it gives the loudness (LUFS) and the highest true peak (dBTP), where the music is last heard against the video's end, and each narration's pace in words a minute against the voice-over speed. These are reported as warnings, each with its time and scene: music that stops more than 1 s before the end, a loop seam (the track's opening heard again), a narration slower than 110 or faster than 180 words a minute, more than 1.5 s of silence inside a narrated scene, a still picture over silence, and a peak above -1 dBTP. The numbers appear in the render's answer (its `Mix` line) and in `get_video`; its findings appear in the renders panel (`Mixed audio`). They never stop a render.

### The clips' own sound

AI shots often come with their own sound - effects, ambience, sometimes music of their own - and an uploaded video carries its own audio. Under the video's music, an AI shot's own sound is off by default, because its music would fight the bed; an uploaded video keeps its sound. Two places change it:

- `Video > Music and sound > The clips' own sound`: `Automatic` (the rule above), `Keep every clip's sound`, or `Off: only the voice and the music` - the music then carries the whole video under the narration.
- A clip scene's own `Its own sound` in the inspector: `Automatic` (the video's choice), `Keep it` or `Off`. The inspector says what the automatic choice does for that scene, and the timeline marks every scene whose own sound plays with a speaker.

The assistant does the same with `clip_sound` on the video and `audio` on a scene, and tells you what plays where after each change, naming the shots that have no sound at all. An AI shot made for a video whose clips' own sound is `Off` is made without sound: the track the model adds anyway is dropped as soon as the shot arrives.

The voice always leads. A clip's sound that plays is levelled toward the voice (a loud shot brought down, a quiet ambience left quiet), set by its `Volume` against the voice (0.8 by default), faded in and out over the transitions around it, and lowered by 10 dB under every sentence, like the music. Under a narration the voice sits on top, the clip's sound under it, the music under both.

The final soundtrack is normalised to -16 LUFS, the loudness streaming platforms expect for speech-led video.

Captions are drawn on the video (or not: `Burn them into the video`) and always delivered as a WebVTT file next to it.

## Media and their retention

The `Media` tab of the Video Studio lists your recordings and AI shots together, newest first and a page at a time, with the videos that use each one; the page bar sits above and below the grid, like on the `Videos` tab. Search them by name or prompt, and filter them by kind, by use (`In a video` or `In no video`) and by when they were made (a preset such as `Last 7 days`, or a From / To day): the search and the filters cover your whole library, not only the page shown. "In no video" means no video at all uses it, a colleague's included: a card used only by a video you cannot open says `In a video you cannot open`.

To clean the library up, select media and delete them together: tick cards one by one (across pages), tick the box of the bar above the grid for the whole page, then `select all` for every medium the filters match. `Select all unused` does it in one click for every recording and AI shot no video uses. A medium counts as used when any video needs it: a scene plays it, a screen insert, a backdrop or an image shows it, an AI shot the video plays was made from it (its first frame, the recording or shot that frame came from, its reference pictures), or a ready render of the video drew it. A deletion of several media never deletes one a video uses: those are kept, the confirmation and the result say how many, and deleting one of them anyway is done from its own card, which names the videos and asks you to type a code back. The use is checked again as the deletion runs, so a medium placed in a video meanwhile is kept, and only your own media are ever deleted.

A recording or a shot that failed or was cancelled made nothing: it is not listed, and once no video uses it, it is deleted an hour after it failed. Their footage is the heaviest part of the studio, so the footage no video uses is kept for a while, not forever:

- **the media a video uses are never deleted**, however long the video is left untouched: it can always be edited and rendered again;
- once no video uses a recording or a shot any more (removed from the timeline, or the video deleted), its media are deleted some time later (30 days by default);
- the media of one no video ever used are deleted some time after they were made (14 days by default);
- **a rendered video keeps its own MP4**: deleting a recording's footage never touches a video already rendered from it;
- the card of a recording or a shot no video uses says when its media expire; `Keep its media` exempts a recording or a shot;
- an expired recording or shot stays listed, marked `Media expired`, with its name, poster and script, so it can be recorded or generated again; a video that still uses it can no longer be rendered until the scene is replaced - or, for an AI shot, until `Generate again` in the inspector makes it again in place, for every video using it.

Deleting a recording or a shot from the `Media` tab removes it and its footage for good. One that no video uses is deleted after a plain confirmation. **One that a video uses is protected**: the confirmation names the videos that use it (and counts the ones you cannot open), and `Delete anyway` stays disabled until you type back the code it shows. The API refuses such a deletion too (`409 media_in_use`) unless it is forced.

If a medium a video uses is deleted anyway, the video keeps opening, previewing and saving: the scene that played it shows `This recording was deleted` (or `This AI shot was deleted`) where it plays, and the rest of the video plays around it. Record it again or give the scene another recording or shot in the inspector; until then the video cannot be rendered, and the render names the scene.

Administrators set both periods in the deployment configuration (see [Video Studio settings](../deployment/configuration.md#video-studio)).

## Render and watch

Render at the quality you need from the editor's `Renders` tab: `Draft` (720p, 30 fps) to check the cut, `High` (1080p, 60 fps, the default) or `Ultra` (4K, 60 fps). A render takes a few minutes and continues if you leave the page; keep editing meanwhile, the render works from the revision saved when it started. A video has one render in progress at a time. A render queued behind another one says how long the render being made has left and when this one should start ("the render being made ends in about 18 min (by its measured pace) - this one starts in about 18 min"), the renders queued before it counted at their estimates. Asking again for a render of the same saved version at the same quality - from the editor, another tab or the assistant - follows the render already waiting or running instead of starting over, and the history shows it once. A render of a newer version or at another quality cancels the one still waiting or running, which stays in the history marked `Replaced by a newer render` - except when the newer version changes nothing the render draws (a screen insert's legibility, a deck's speaker notes, a clip's own sound or its level): saving it keeps the render in progress, which finishes as the render of the newer version, its sound mixed for that version, and rendering that version at the same quality follows it rather than starting over. When a render of an older version is still in progress, the `Renders` tab asks once before it cancels it ("The render of revision 99 is 35% done. Cancel it and render revision 100?"); `Keep it drawing` leaves it to finish. The assistant cancels it without asking and says so. Either way the new render keeps the frames the older one already drew of the scenes the newer version leaves unchanged - the same frames at the same place in the video, at the same quality - and draws only the rest; once ready, its notes say how many frames it kept from which render, scene by scene. A scene moved in time (a scene before it got longer or shorter) is drawn again. Once a render is ready, rendering again a version that changes nothing it shows or plays (a screen insert's legibility, a deck's speaker notes) starts nothing: the `Renders` tab says `Already rendered` and that render becomes the render of the newer version, unless you choose `Render anyway`; a change to a scene's trim or to a clip's own sound renders again, and so does a render drawn before the platform changed how it draws screen inserts or judged before its quality checks changed (a new check, a drawing fix that comes with one), the answer saying so (`drawn at screen-insert drawing version 7, now 8: rendering again`, `judged under quality report v48, now v49: rendering again`), with no `Render anyway` needed. The video's render list says which drawing version and which quality report each render was made under. A render that stops moving for two minutes is restarted where it stood, the frames it had already made kept, and its progress says so; after two such restarts it fails. When the platform is updated or restarted, the worker stops taking new renders and lets a render past half its frames finish, for up to 20 minutes where the deployment gives its workers that long to stop (`VIDEO_STUDIO_RENDER_DRAIN_SECONDS`); a render under half its frames goes to a running worker at once. One still under way is handed to the next worker, first in the queue, which resumes it from the frames already made and says whether it resumed after an update or a restart: an update never fails a render. A render handed over keeps its place on the renderers: a running worker keeps a slot free for it, and when the renderers' slots were all taken meanwhile by renders queued after it, the one that started last gives its slot back - keeping its frames and its place in the queue - so the render handed over resumes within a minute. A worker that dies without stopping (out of memory, a crash) is noticed within seconds, its render taken back first in the queue and resumed, and the render says how the worker went - the signal and the memory it used then. The render list and the assistant say how long a render left by a stopped worker has waited and how long it may still wait: as long as an update of the platform can take to replace its worker - 10 minutes unless the deployment says otherwise (`WORKER_TERMINATION_GRACE_SECONDS`, `WORKER_STARTUP_SECONDS`) -, and for as long as another render holds the slot it waits for. A scene preview holds that slot only when it runs in a render slot lent to it, and gives it back to the waiting render, which takes it first; previews on their own slots are listed apart and never make it wait. Once its slot frees, the render still has its whole wait before it fails. One that no worker resumes by then fails instead of showing as running, saying which workers were running and why none took it. Render it again at the same quality within a day: the same render is queued again first and resumes from the frames it had already made, instead of starting over. Before it starts, the assistant says how long the render should take and what that estimate is made of: the frames, the seconds a frame takes on each page of the renderer and where that figure comes from (the platform's past renders at that quality, its renders at other qualities, or a reference measurement when it has none), the pages, the CPUs and the memory. The frames are counted by what they show - one scene, two scenes during a transition, or a screen insert, which costs a page many times a plain frame - and by the type of scene they belong to - a clip with a screen insert, a statement over a clip, a figure -, each at the time a page took for a frame of that scene type on the platform's past renders and scene previews (by its kind only for a type never drawn here; first at the time the renders on the same renderer - its pages, CPUs and memory - took for it at that quality; and, when the video was already rendered at that quality, every scene type at the time its last render took for it, its pages sharing the frames as that render's pages did over the whole run), with the time the screen inserts' preparation and the checks of the finished video take, and the narrations at the length they were spoken when they already were. The estimate is quoted from the slowest scene type the video holds: how many frames it has and what one takes a page, since the page drawing those scenes decides how long the render takes. It says how much one page of the renderer holds at its peak (measured on a 4K page drawing screen inserts, for `Ultra`), how many pages the renderer's memory opens, and how long the render takes on them against on every page, with the memory to give for every page. It also says what its last estimates at that quality on the same renderer said against what those renders took - up to the last five made the same way, named - and corrects itself by that; an estimate made from the video's own render is corrected only by estimates made that way. It counts only what the render will draw: an animated figure an earlier render of the video already drew, unchanged - at that quality or sharper - is reused rather than drawn again, and the estimate says how many. The renderer's pages share the frames by what they cost, and a page done with its share takes frames from the pages with the most left, so the frames of a long screen insert are drawn by every page rather than by the one or two that started them. While the frames are drawn, the render's progress says how many frames are left, how many it drew a second over the last minute and how long the rest should take. The render's card in chat and in the `Renders` tab says when it should end: as estimated, then, once a tenth of its frames are drawn, at the pace they measured, which the assistant says too instead of the estimate. When the frames draw at under half the estimate's pace for more than 2 minutes, the progress says why, and the assistant says it in full: a scene type costing more than it was counted at ("figure 2.90 s a frame, expected 0.32 s"), how many of the screen-insert frames were read from the renderer's cache, the other jobs running on the same renderer (scene previews, another render's checks or music) and the share of the renderer's CPUs the render got. Under a third of the pace for 5 minutes, the render also checks once whether the renderer's CPU quota is holding it back and how busy the render lanes are, and its progress says so. Once it is drawn, the assistant says what a frame of each scene type took against what the estimate counted, which the next estimates count instead; a render slowed that way corrects no later estimate, and one slowed by a busy renderer teaches the next estimates of its video only the scene types it found too costly. An estimate made from the video's own last render says which scene types that render could not price (a scene added since, or one drawn on a busy renderer) and prices them at what the platform measured for their type. A whole render keeps its renderer's CPUs: scene previews, and the encoding of a recording, run at background priority and take only the CPU the render leaves, and while a render draws, its renderer starts one new preview at a time beside it (others go to a renderer with room, or wait). A recording itself captures at the render's priority, since it follows a live page in real time. While other jobs share its renderer, the render's progress says the share of the CPUs it gets ("its pages get 7.2 of 8 CPUs beside 2 scene previews"). Before it starts, the estimate counts the previews and recordings running or queued on the renderer, and the platform's other jobs running there (assignment runs, sub-agent tasks), each at the CPU that renderer measured them using, and says both times: "about 31 min on a worker shared with 5 scene previews, 1 recording and 6 assignment jobs (about 28 min on an idle worker)". A whole render draws at a higher weight than every other job of its renderer where the deployment allows it (see [Sizing the workers](../deployment/configuration.md#sizing-the-workers)), and the estimate says whether it does; while a renderer draws a render, new assignment runs go to a worker drawing none first. Once drawn, the render also says what the rest of its renderer used, the CPUs left unused and how long its pages waited for one: pages that left CPUs unused without waiting were held back by what they drew, not by the jobs beside them. While a render draws, the assistant lists the jobs sharing its renderer, with who started each and its video (when you can open it), and offers to cancel those that are yours; it asks you first. Once drawn, a render's time counts toward the next estimates at the share of the CPUs it got. Once drawn, the assistant says how many frames the render drew and how many it kept from an earlier attempt, so a render that resumed after an update is never mistaken for a faster one. A render one renderer cannot finish within 4 hours - or, when the platform runs several renderers, within 30 minutes on the pages its memory opens (`VIDEO_STUDIO_RENDER_SPLIT_MINUTES`) - is cut into scene ranges, each drawn by a renderer of its own, then joined with its sound once: with several renderers, a long `Ultra` video renders in a fraction of the time. A range that no other renderer takes is drawn by the render's own renderer after its range, so the render never waits for a renderer the platform does not have. An `Ultra` render the renderers cannot hold (too little memory, too slow even split into ranges) is refused until the platform has rendered at that quality once. The refusal says how long the render would take, the CPUs or memory a renderer needs, and the highest quality that fits now, for you to choose. You can also render anyway. A second render of the same version at another quality reuses what does not depend on the quality: the narrations, the figures drawn by a sharper render, the music composed for the video and the screen inserts followed through their footage. The result is an MP4 (H.264 video, AAC sound) with a poster image and the captions file. The poster is a settled frame of the first scene after the title card, never a frame caught in the middle of a transition. When the captions are burned into the video, the player leaves the captions file off, so they never show twice; when they are not, the player shows the file in the band the scenes keep clear for it.

To check one scene you changed without rendering the whole video, ask the assistant to preview it ("preview the booth scene"): it renders just that scene with its voice, its music and its transitions in and out, usually in under a minute, and shows you the video and its first, middle and last frames - the scene settled, out of its transitions and of the video's fade from and to black, an opening or closing card your template adds included. A scene showing a figure, a recording or a screen insert is previewed at 1080p (30 fps), so you judge its sharpness on what a render shows; any other scene is a 720p draft (ask for a 1080p preview of it if you want one). A screen insert's frames are drawn once per worker too: a preview of its scene after another change (a title, a narration, a scene beside it), or a render of the video at the same size, reads them back instead of drawing them again, so it takes about the time of a scene without an insert. A preview hears and grades its own narration only - its neighbours' words, which play in its transitions, are left to their own previews and to the render. A player scales a preview to its screen, so a 720p draft looks softer on a large display than the render will: the assistant says the size each preview was drawn at, and the editor's `Renders` tab lists the video's scene previews with that size under each one, to play them full screen. Scene previews run beside the whole renders, on worker slots of their own: a preview never waits for a 4K render, and a render never waits for anyone's previews. The worker keeps memory for its previews that no render takes, so a preview always draws at its full speed: when its worker is already busy with other previews beside a render, it goes to another worker with room, or waits for the others to end, and its answer says which. While a worker has no whole render to make and its preview slots are all busy, its render slot takes the next preview too: a whole render asked meanwhile waits for that one preview to end. A preview never replaces the video's current render, and is kept for a day. Ask for as many previews as you like - every scene of the video at once if you want: each one is queued, a few of one video run at a time, and each free slot takes the videos in turn, one preview each - the video with the fewest previews being made first, then the one served longest ago - so a sweep of twenty scenes never waits behind every preview of another film, and the preview of a video asked after another's whole sweep comes next. A preview waiting for one worker's memory is taken at once by another worker whose slot frees, in its turn. While several videos have previews in the queue, each video runs at most its share of the preview slots, so one video's sweep never keeps every slot while another video's preview is about to start. While a preview waits, the assistant names what it waits behind: "1 preview of video Launch film ahead", the previews being made included, and how many workers draw previews ("1 worker draws previews, 2 at a time"). It also says how long the preview should wait, from the moment it is queued, and how loaded the lane is: "about 20 min before it starts at the lane's pace (a scene preview drawn in about 90 s, the median of the last 20; the previews started in the last hour waited 25 min at the 95th percentile)"; the job carries the instant it is expected to start. A preview that waited behind other videos' previews on a platform with a single preview worker says once, for the administrator, that a second preview-only worker would start another video's preview at once. The assistant also reads the preview lane as the last hour measured it - its workers and slots, the previews queued, the median and 95th-percentile wait in the queue and the median time a preview takes to draw - and when that 95th-percentile wait passes twice the median draw time, that the lane is too small for what is asked of it: add a preview-only worker, or raise the preview slots of a worker whose CPUs are mostly idle. An edit of only the video's theme (its watermark, its backdrop, its colours) or its music keeps the preview every scene passed: no scene needs a new preview for it, and the whole render draws and checks the new look. A figure is drawn once and kept with the video: the previews of the scenes around it, and a second preview of its own scene, reuse it rather than drawing it again, and a scene beside a figure draws only the moment of the figure its transition shows. A scene previewed again at the same quality with nothing it shows or says changed - the scene, the ones around it, its narrations, the look, the template - has every frame drawn already: it never waits behind other previews, and is ready in seconds, its answer saying so ("744 of 744 frames cached, encoded in 1.4 s"). A screen insert on a scene before or after the one previewed is drawn as in that scene's own preview; if its source cannot be read, the preview fails saying which, rather than showing the scene without it - and so it does for a missing backdrop or AI shot of a scene beside it, with the reason that scene's own preview would give. The assistant says where a preview stands in its lane of the queue, and behind which videos' previews. A preview is made exactly as a whole render makes that scene, and gets the same sound and picture checks (below), so you can validate a scene before rendering the whole video. The narrations of the scenes before and after it play in its transitions and are heard back too: one that fails is a warning on that neighbouring scene, never a failure of the scene previewed. So is what the picture check finds on a neighbour's screen insert shown through a transition: it stays on the scene that owns the insert, a warning that names it (or left out, when that scene's own preview already reported it), never a failure of the scene previewed - except the key showing on its screen, or the insert missing, on a frame of the transition itself: that frame is the preview's own, so it fails it. The answer gives the neighbour's insert's numbers over the transition's frames, measured there, never left to that scene's own preview. Whether that insert sits on its screen's bezel is the one number a preview does not read again at another size: a 720p draft beside a scene whose own 1080p preview, or the save, measured it gives their measure as a fact ("0.02% of the frame's width off its bezel, as save revision 9 measured it ... at 1920x1080"), never a finding.

### Every render is heard and watched back

- **Each narration is heard back before it is placed.** Once spoken, a narration is transcribed and compared with its text. It fails when more than one word in ten differs, or when the speech repeats words the text does not. A name with no pronunciation entry (a capitalized word such as `Moscone Center`) counts as heard when what was transcribed sounds like it, since speech-to-text spells a name the way it heard it. A narration that fails is first given the take of the same voice that already passed on the same words, for example in the scene's preview. Without one, it is spoken again with the same voice; when those two takes got the same pronunciation term wrong, the next one says that term differently - by how its entry says it, set apart by commas - rather than sending the same sentence a third time; then once more a little slower (85% of the video's pace) with the pronunciations written into its text, then with the voice of the same speech model closest to the video's - the one sharing the most of what the provider says of its voices (accent, gender, tone...), never another provider's or the platform's default voice. A narration that fails every time is never placed silently: the render stops and says which scene and why. A narration that passed is not checked again in later renders.
- **A slower take is checked again when the voice check improves.** A narration that only passed slower, or with a term set apart, is listed after each save with what would let it pass at the video's speed (a pronunciation entry the voice says right, or a reworded sentence). Once that pronunciation entry changes, or a new release changes how narrations are checked, asking for a render again no longer answers that the video is already rendered: it offers to re-check that narration alone. Accepted (and at once in an automation), only that narration is spoken and heard again; if it now passes at the video's speed, its sound is placed into a new render that keeps the previous render's pictures, so no frame is drawn again unless the new take changes what is shown (a scene whose length or captions follow its narration). Asking for a re-check yourself ("render it again and re-check the slower takes") does the same for every narration that plays a slower take, whether or not anything changed: each is spoken again at the video's speed, past the cached takes, and heard with the same check as the voice-over preview; the answer says, for each, the speed and voice it was spoken with, the share of its words heard as written, and whether the slower take was replaced or kept. When no narration plays one, it says that no re-check was performed. A render that plays a slower take without hearing it again says that its figure comes from the check that first chose that take.
- **Another voice is refused like a failed narration.** When only that closest voice says a narration right, the audience would hear two narrators in one video. The render stops before drawing, as it does for a narration that failed, with a failure for that scene (`Narration spoken by another voice`) naming the voice that said it, the video's voice, why it failed and what to change; the other scenes are untouched. A scene preview plays that voice so you can hear it, with the same failure; the quality check shows it too, as a finding the audience would notice, and `Render` refuses the video for that scene until the video's own voice passes. Reword the narration or add a pronunciation, then preview the scene to try the video's own voice again: once it passes, the video renders as usual. To keep the other voice, make it the video's voice, or render anyway: the video is rendered with it, and the finding stays on the render.
- **A scene preview checks the narration the whole video uses.** A preview speaks each narration exactly as the whole video does, with the narrations around it, so a narration that passed in a preview is placed as it is in the next render, without being spoken or checked again.
- **The voice-over has its own clean track.** Every narration is placed at its scene's start, to the sample, with a short fade at each end. Two narrations never overlap: a scene lasts as long as its narration.
- **The finished video is checked.** Once the video is assembled, its soundtrack is played back and compared with the voice-over track. The render fails when more than one word in ten of the narration is heard wrong, when a sentence is heard twice, or when the same fraction of a second of sound repeats three times in a row (a stutter). The picture is watched too: any stretch of over half a second where the frame does not change is reported - a locked-off shot where only a small subject moves (a cat far down a corridor), even one barely lighter than the wall, is not frozen while it moves; a shot carrying a screen insert is watched in parts, its footage and each insert apart, so a static page on a screen in a room where a candle flickers, or a still room under a page that scrolls, is not frozen, and a frozen picture says which part stopped moving - except where stillness is the design - a crossfade or a fade through black, where the picture holds while it fades, title cards, closing cards and statements, an image or a slide set to no motion, an animated figure resting between its steps, a recording while the product it shows is at rest (its held end, after the recording, is checked), and your own `Hold the last frame` at the end of a scene. A frozen picture is a warning: the render is ready. Every frame of a recording scene is also compared with the frames on either side of it, in a render and in a scene preview: a frame drawn as a mix of the two (the page before showing as a ghost over the page after, its text drawn twice) is reported as a double image, with its time, its scene and that frame stored with the render, wherever it was made (a slowed scene, a camera move, a held end); a crossfade, a fade through black or any transition into the scene is never one. A double image is a warning held like a frozen picture: a scene's preview clears it. The music is measured as it was mixed (see "Music" above): its levels are shown under the render in `Renders`, and a quiet mix is a warning too. While the finished video is checked, its progress says how many narrations have been heard and how far into the picture the check has watched - ten seconds at a time, naming the stretch and the scenes it is on -, so a long check is never taken for a stuck one. A stretch the check cannot judge within a minute is skipped and listed with the render's checks as not judged, with its times and scenes: the check never holds the render back. Title and closing cards (your template's included) and statements are never compared frame by frame: how long their picture holds is listed as by design, never as a frozen picture. A render stopped while it is checked resumes with every frame it had encoded and goes on watching where it stood.
- **The finished video hears each narration where it plays.** Each narration is transcribed alone, over the stretch of the voice-over track where its take was placed, and only where that track carries a voice: the music-only moments of a scene are never transcribed. What the speech-to-text is known to write over silence or music (whisper-1's "Diolch am wylio'r fideo", "Thanks for watching"), or what it hears in another language than the voice-over's, is left out and counted with the render's checks, never as words heard wrong - unless the narration says those words. A scene counts at most all of its words as wrong. When a narration passed its own check and the finished video hears it differently, the finding is a warning naming both transcripts: the narration's own check decides its words. A narration the voice-over track does not play at all still fails. A pronunciation term the finished video misses at its place is looked for up to 3 seconds before and after it first, and passes where it is heard.
- **Every finding has a time.** Each finding says where it is in the video (`1:02.35`) and in which scene. They are listed under the render in `Renders` and in the assistant's answers. A render that failed its sound check keeps its video so you can play it at those times; it never becomes the video's current render.
- **A render's answer starts with its verdict.** The assistant's answer for a render or a scene preview says first whether it is green (nothing blocks or warns) or blocked. It then lists, apart, the findings (each with its time and what to change), what a setting you chose shows by design (the bands a screen insert set to `Fit inside the frame` leaves on a wider surface, its colours at the light you gave it), and the measurements that block nothing. Only findings are issues: a render whose findings are empty passes every gate. Advice on sizing the platform's workers is for the administrator, and is said once, in the first render that needs it; so is a defect of the render itself, with its frame.

Speech-to-text uses the same OpenAI provider as dictation in the chat: the narrations' audio is sent to it to be transcribed, whichever provider speaks them, within the providers each person may use. Each narration is transcribed once: the check of the finished video reuses what was heard when the narration was checked, as long as the encoded voice still plays that very take, and transcribes again only a narration the stitching or the encoding changed, or one checked before this was kept. The render's checks name the speech-to-text model and provider (the provider's name as set in Settings > AI Models). Without one, the narrations are placed unchecked and the render says so. The stutter and frozen-picture checks still run.

A clip, a recording or an animated image shorter than its narration keeps moving to the end of its scene by default: it plays slower to fill it, then drifts in on its last frame (see "Footage keeps moving to the end of its scene" above). A scene's `When the narration outlasts it` setting can instead play it at its own speed and then loop its last second with a crossfade (clips and animated images only), or play it at its own speed and freeze its last frame. A frozen last frame is reported when you save: the quality check compares the clip's or the recording's length (its trim, its speed) with its narration - at the length it was spoken, or at the reading pace until it is - and a scene set to freeze whose last frame would stay still on screen for more than half a second is a finding that stops the render until you change it, suggesting the drift or the loop instead, so no render is paid for to report it.

**The render checks before it starts.** `Render` refuses, with its reasons, when an AI shot or a recording the video plays is still being made, failed or expired (each with what a render would do with it), when the quality check saved for the video finds something the audience would see (advice alone never stops it), when a narration failed its last check, when the previous render of the same revision failed its sound check (the same revision would sound the same), when the previous render at the same quality showed a frozen picture (a draft that showed one never stops the final render), or when a scene changed since it was last previewed or rendered - its words, what its preview shows of the scene before or after it (their picture and the transitions between them, not their narration), the video's look or its music, its transition (its own, or the video's default when it has none), whether a clip keeps its own sound, the video's fade on its first or last scene, what a pronunciation applied to the captions shows, or a new take of the recording or AI shot it shows. Editing what one scene shows marks at most three scenes as changed (it and its neighbours), rewording its narration only that scene (unless it changes a transition beside it); the others keep their preview. What counts is what a scene shows, not when it was saved: put a scene back as it was, even after previewing the change, and the preview made of it before counts again (the save says so). What draws nothing never marks a scene: adding or removing the video's references, or the references a shot is held to, keeps every preview, and so does a platform update that adds a setting left at its default. Each refused scene says what changed in it (its narration, an overlay's corners, its transition in, the scene before it), and the save and the video's details list the scenes whose preview still holds. A save that changes nothing at all is not saved: no new revision, nothing to preview again. A scene nobody previewed yet never holds a render back, and neither does a change of voice, delivery, pace or of how the voice says a term: the render hears every narration again before it draws anything, and stops on one that fails. A preview that passes is trusted: a finding about an AI shot (its camera moved, for instance) no longer holds the render once every scene showing that shot was previewed as it is now and passed, nor does what an earlier render found in a scene previewed since, so a video whose every scene preview passed renders without being forced. You can render anyway: the choice is recorded on the render and in the audit log.

**The assistant renders a video once it is ready.** Refused, the assistant fixes what it can, waits for the shots, previews the changed scenes all at once (the previews run side by side) and looks at their frames, then renders. Ask it to render anyway and it does (`force`).

Renders wait in a queue for a free renderer, and each one says where it stands (being made, how many ahead of it). One person can have a limited number of renders queued or running at once, across all their videos (10 by default, `VIDEO_STUDIO_MAX_RENDERS_PER_USER`); past it, the render is refused with the ones in flight listed, so you can wait for one or cancel one (from its video's `Renders` tab, or by asking the assistant, which can also list everything of yours still being made).

When the assistant starts a render - or a recording, an AI shot or an AI track - that is still running when its answer ends, it comes back to the conversation once the work is done, without being asked: it shows the rendered video, says what failed and why, and carries on with your request if a step of it remains. When it started several at once, it comes back once, when the last of them ends. Stopping the conversation keeps it quiet: the work still finishes, and the assistant says nothing until you write again.

Each render runs on its own, within a memory budget the administrator sets (`VIDEO_STUDIO_RENDER_MEMORY_MB`), so a render that fails never stops anything else the platform is doing. A render that crashes, or whose renderer stopped answering for a moment, starts again by itself at the same quality, up to three attempts in all, and picks up where it stopped: the frames and the animated figures it had finished are kept, and only the rest is drawn. Frames are kept only when everything they were drawn from is the same - each scene, each file, each screen insert's every setting down to its key's tolerance and feather -, so a video edited meanwhile is drawn again, and the render's answer says which frames it kept, for which saved version and by which attempt (or that it kept none: a preview or a render never takes another render's frames). A render that stops moving forward for two minutes is resumed the same way, twice at most. A render that is still drawing is never failed because its renderer checked in late: it starts again from the frames it had finished, even after its last attempt. When it fails for good, or uses more memory than its budget, it says why: how far it got, which scenes it was drawing and how much memory it used. The video and its quality need no change: the way through is more memory for the worker, which the administrator gives. A failure of the platform rather than of the video - its headless browser failing, its memory, a crash, a stall, its worker going silent - first runs the render again once, by itself, while the video has not changed since, keeping the frames it had finished. A render that still fails says the failure was the platform's, that it already ran again once, and that nothing in the video needs to change. A failure of the platform rather than of the video - its headless browser failing, its memory, a crash, a stall, its worker going silent - first runs the render again once, by itself, while the video has not changed since, keeping the frames it had finished. A render that still fails says the failure was the platform's, that it already ran again once, and that nothing in the video needs to change. While a render runs, the assistant can see when its renderer last checked in, and says so when it is late.

Each render that finishes becomes the video's **current render**: the one the library card, the chat card and the public link play. To play an earlier one instead (the 1080p cut rather than the draft you rendered after it), open its menu in `Renders` and choose `Make it the current render`.

Rendering changes what the video plays, so it is for the video's editors. Someone the video is shared with as a viewer duplicates it to render a version of their own.

## Watch a video

`Play` opens the video over the whole window, on black, and in your browser's full screen at once - never in a small box. It is in the editor's header (the current render), on each render in `Renders`, on a rendered video's card in the library (the play button on its picture, or `Play` in its menu), and on every recording and AI shot in `Media`. The title, what is playing and `Download` sit at the top; `Esc` leaves the browser's full screen, and `Esc` again, or `Close`, takes you back where you were.

The player is the same everywhere a video plays - there, on a viewer's page, on the video's card in a conversation and on its public link: play and pause, 10 seconds back and forward, a timeline that shows what is loaded and the time under the pointer, the volume (remembered from one video to the next), the captions on or off when they are not burned into the picture, the speed (0.5x to 2x), loop, picture-in-picture and full screen. While the video plays, the controls and the pointer step aside after a moment; move the pointer, or tap the video on a phone, to bring them back. Click the picture to play or pause, double-click it for full screen; on a phone or a tablet, double-tap its left or right side to go 10 seconds back or forward (tap again to go further).

- **Chapters.** A render made since chapters exist has one per scene: the timeline is cut where each scene takes over, the pointer over it names the scene, and the chapters button next to the time names the one playing and lists them all with where they start, to jump to one. A scene is named as on the editor's timeline - a card's title, a statement's first line, the recording's or the AI shot's name - else by its place and type (`Scene 3 - AI shot`). Your keyboard's media keys and your phone's lock screen play, pause and skip the video too, and their previous / next track keys move between chapters.
- **Where you left it.** Leave a video before its end and it starts where you stopped the next time you play it in this browser, with `Start over` for a few seconds. A video watched to its end starts from the beginning.
- **When the connection drops.** The player picks the video up where it was once it can; if it really cannot play it, it says so with `Try again`.

Press `?` in the player for the list of its keys; it is also in the menu behind the speed (`1x`), with `Loop`.

| Key | Does |
|---|---|
| `Space` / `K` | Play / pause |
| `Left` / `Right` | 5 seconds back / forward |
| `J` / `L` | 10 seconds back / forward |
| `Up` / `Down` | Volume up / down |
| `M` | Mute / unmute |
| `C` | Captions on / off |
| `F` | Full screen |
| `0` to `9` | Jump to that tenth of the video |
| `Home` / `End` | To the start / the end |
| `<` / `>` | Slower / faster |
| `,` / `.` | Previous / next frame (the video pauses) |
| `?` | The list of the player's keys |
| `Esc` | Leave full screen, then close the player |

## Download

`Download`, at the top right of the editor, saves the current render: the MP4 (its quality and size are shown), the captions as a WebVTT file and the poster image. The files are named after the video and its quality, for example `Launch film - 1080p60.mp4`. The same entries are in the menu of the video's card in the library and on its card in a conversation, and every render in `Renders` downloads its own files. Until the first render is ready, `Download` says so.

## Share a video

`Share`, at the top right of the editor (or in the menu of the video's card in the library), works like the presentation one:

- **People and groups.** Give access to people with an account here, or to groups, as `Can view` (watch, download and duplicate) or `Can edit` (also edit, render and share, in the editor or through their own assistant).
- **The company.** An administrator can publish the video to everyone in the company; only administrators then change it.
- **The link to copy.** The video's address opens it for the people it is shared with, and nobody else: the dialog says who it opens for before you send it. `Copy link` in the editor's menu copies it too.

Ask your assistant to do it - "share this video with ada@example.com as an editor" - and it changes the sharing with the dialog's rules: only the owner or an editor shares, and only with existing accounts. Every change, from the dialog or the assistant, is in the audit log with who gained access, who lost it and whose access changed.

The library lists your videos, those shared with you and those of the company, the last changed first and a page at a time, with a filter for each and a search that covers the whole library (title, template or owner). The page, the filter and the search stay in the address, so a link or a reload keeps them. A globe marks a video with a public link, an eye one you can only view.

## Public link

For people who have no account here - a customer, a partner, an event audience - turn on the video's **public link** in the `Share` dialog. Anyone who has the link watches the video's current render in their browser, without signing in, in the whole window with the same player (see [Watch a video](#watch-a-video)): never its scenes, recordings, AI shots or editor, and nothing about who made it or who it is shared with. A new render, or another render made current, is what the link plays next.

- `Copy public link` copies it; `Replace link` issues a new one, and the old one stops working at once.
- `Allow download` (off on a new link) gives its visitors a download button for the MP4 and its captions. It is a convenience, not a protection: anyone who can watch the video can save what the player plays, with the option off as well.
- Turning the link off makes it answer "not available" at once; the people and groups the video is shared with keep their access.
- Until the video is rendered, the link says the video is not ready yet.
- One address can pull at most 2 GiB of the video's files per minute - some thirty viewers of a 1080p60 render behind one office address - past which the link answers "too many requests" until the minute is over.

Your assistant can turn the link on or off and return it: "give me a public link to this video". A public link is a capability: whoever has the address can watch the video, and can pass it on, so turn it off or replace it when it is no longer needed. Administrators can turn public video links off for the whole platform in **Settings > Access and security > Security options** (`Public video links`, or `VIDEO_PUBLIC_LINKS_ENABLED=false`, see [Security options](../administration/settings.md#security-options)): every link then stops working at once and none can be created; turning the option back on restores the links that were not turned off.

## Render retention

Every render keeps its own MP4, poster, captions and frames, so a video's renders are kept for a while, not forever. Once a night, a render is deleted with its files when nothing keeps it and its days are over:

- a **failed or cancelled** render, 1 day after it ended;

A whole render that fails at one of its checks after its frames were drawn (a picture gate, the frozen-picture watch, any quality check on the finished video) also keeps its drawn frames for 24 hours (`RENDER_FAILED_KEEP_SECONDS = 86400`). While they are kept, the render says `frames kept until <UTC time>` in `Renders` and in the assistant's answer. The next render of the same revision at the same quality, drawn from exactly the same inputs, resumes from them: it draws only the segments missing, its frozen-picture watch goes on where the failed one stood, and its notes say `Resumed from kept frames of render <id> at <s> s`. A revision that changes what is drawn ignores them and they are deleted; past 24 hours the hourly cleanup deletes them. A successful render keeps no frames beyond its end.

- a **scene preview**, 7 days after it ended - and as soon as a new preview of the same scene is ready, which replaces it;
- an **older ready render**, one a newer ready render of the same quality replaced, 30 days after it ended.

Never deleted: the **newest ready render of each quality** (your newest 1080p cut and your newest 4K, say), the **current render** - the one the library card, the chat card and the public link play - a render in progress, and a render you kept with `Keep it` in its menu. Administrators set the days (see [Render retention](../administration/settings.md#render-retention)). Each render in `Renders` says what keeps it or until when it is kept.

## Clean up renders

To free the space at once, `Clean up renders` in `Renders` deletes many renders together, with their files. Choose what goes:

- `Everything but the newest ready render of each quality` (the default), or keep only the newest render of one quality - your 1080p final, say - and let a 4K draft go too;
- `Failed and cancelled renders`;
- `Scene previews`;
- `Everything, the newest ready renders too`, only once you confirm it: the video then has nothing to play until it is rendered again.

`Only renders made before` limits the cleanup to older renders. The dialog lists every render the cleanup would delete, keep or refuse, and why, with what it frees, before anything is deleted. A render still in progress is refused (cancel it first), and so is the render the public link plays, unless you confirm it: the link then plays the newest ready render left. On a video shared with you to watch only, you can delete only the renders you asked for. Frames you saved in a conversation stay. `Delete`, in a render's menu, still removes a single render.

Your assistant does the same when you ask ("delete the old renders of this video, keep the 1080p final"): it can list what would go first, and says what it deleted, how much space it freed and which render the video plays now. Every cleanup is in the audit log.

## Delete a video

Deleting a video in the Video Studio deletes it for good: there is no trash. Its renders and scene previews go with it, with their files; its recordings and AI shots stay in your library, and the ones no other video uses are deleted after their retention days (see [Media and their retention](#media-and-their-retention)).

Your assistant deletes a video only when you ask it to ("delete the video *Launch film*"), and only a video you own (an administrator for a company-managed one): a video shared with you is refused. When other people open the video (shared with people or groups, or with a public link on), it asks you to confirm first, since deleting it removes it for them too. A card of the video in a conversation then reads that it is no longer available. Every deletion is in the audit log.

Administrators find every video, whoever made it, under `Administration > Objects` (filter `Videos`), see [Objects](../administration/introduction.md#objects). A video posted in a conversation on another channel (Slack, Teams, email) is a link to its page.

## Limits and settings

Administrators set the recording and video length limits, the number of renders that run at once, the media retention and the ffmpeg location in the deployment configuration (see [Video Studio settings](../deployment/configuration.md#video-studio)), and the render retention in `Settings` (see [Render retention](../administration/settings.md#render-retention)).
